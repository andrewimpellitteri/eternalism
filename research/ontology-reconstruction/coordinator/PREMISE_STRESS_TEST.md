# Preliminary stress test: what can actually select an ontology?

29 October 2026. Coordinator mathematical/argument preparation. Elementary checks, not an independent review, source attribution, novelty claim, or metaphysical verdict. This file is separate from all four worker-owned lanes. The only literature dependencies are the existing project's reviewed scope; the explicit constructions below can be inspected directly.

## 1. Covariance and persistence do not distinguish the two simplest rivals

Use time-oriented globally hyperbolic spacetimes M and marked points b. Consider only candidate event-domain assignments, not complete metaphysical accounts:

    E_all(M,b) = M
    E_past(M,b) = J^-_M(b).

Both are invariant under time-orientation-preserving isometries. Both contain b. If b <= c, then E(M,b) is a subset of E(M,c): trivially for E_all and by causal transitivity for E_past. They also agree with their own restrictions under causally convex open embeddings U -> M:

    E(U,b) = U intersect E(M,b).

For the causal past this uses causal convexity: a causal curve between points of U cannot leave U. For the whole domain it is set identity. Both domains are closed relative to their ambient spacetime (causal simplicity follows here from global hyperbolicity).

Therefore covariance, causal persistence, self-inclusion, closedness, and this restriction condition together do NOT select between these assignments. Taking the domains as concrete inventories is a further interpretation. Adding nonuniversality excludes E_all explicitly; adding future exclusion does so too. Neither can then be advertised as a neutral theorem proving the failure of eternalism.

The lesson is stronger than a missing technical estimate: a desired uniqueness theorem must identify an additional philosophically defended premise, or change its conclusion to a classification that retains both.

## 2. Restriction compatibility can conceal opposition to future-dependent criteria

A concrete same-law example shows why the embedding category matters. In 1+1 Minkowski spacetime with coordinates (t,z), set

    b=(0,-1), x=(0,1), p=(1/2,-1), q=(1/2,1),
    U=I^-(p) union I^-(q).

U is open and a past set. It is connected: the two open past cones overlap sufficiently far in the past. It inherits strong causality. For a,c in U, the ambient causal diamond J^+(a) intersect J^-(c) lies in U because U is a past set; it is compact in Minkowski spacetime and therefore compact in U. Thus U is globally hyperbolic. It is causally convex, again because it is a past set. Its restricted flat metric is a vacuum solution. This is not merely an off-shell deformation.

Both b and x lie in U and are spacelike separated. In M they have a common strict future, for instance (2,0). But every common causal future y=(t,z) obeys

    t >= max(|z+1|, |z-1|) >= 1.

Every point of U has t < 1/2. There is no common future of b,x in U.

Consequently the instantaneous common-future potential criterion, evaluated intrinsically on U, excludes x although its evaluation on M includes x. It fails the displayed restriction equality even for a connected, causally convex, globally hyperbolic flat subspacetime.

This does not refute a common-future ontology. It shows that full restriction equality is a substantive anti-globality premise when membership depends on future witnesses. A rival may evaluate its realm using the ambient universe, restrict the permitted embeddings, or reject equality in favor of one-direction preservation. These changes must be stated. The example does not claim anything about maximal developments, completeness, or intrinsic evaluations on all physically admissible universes.

## 3. The comparison of global and fragment accounts needs a grounding discriminator

Suppose a global model supplies all its pointed causal pasts, each with restricted fields, and records their overlap identifications. Every event is in its own causal past, so these pieces cover M. Local tensors can be recovered from the pieces and their compatible identifications. This elementary reconstruction is not a theorem that all fundamental reality is global, nor that the pieces are fundamental: the same mathematical agreement data can be read in either direction.

In particular, the following are different tasks:

- reconstructing the mathematical model from overlapping parts;
- deriving which represented entities concretely exist;
- explaining whether global structure grounds fragments or compatible fragments ground global structure.

A model-reconstruction theorem accomplishes the first. A positive ontology needs an argument for the second and/or third. Counting reconstructed objects or invoking uniqueness of the reconstructed model will not do that work.

## 4. Provisional argument to investigate, with its weakest point exposed

Candidate argument, not accepted:

1. Ontological distinctions require either a basis in physical structure or an independently defensible explanatory role.
2. A candidate preferred-present/actualization structure has neither in the specified physical category.
3. We should not posit that structure as fundamental.

This can support a preference only if premise 2 is established for a concrete candidate and premise 1 is defended. Premise 1 is an explanatory norm, not a physical theorem. Premise 2 cannot follow from empirical inactivity alone. A rival may cite grounding, persistence, experience or modal structure as the explanatory role; those responses require examination. An eternalist's interpretation that every represented event exists is subject to exactly the same requirement.

Even if successful, this argument against one added structure does not select eternalism over every local or later-grounded alternative. The target should be a conditional preference between fully specified rivals, not elimination by labels.

## 5. Required coordinator questions at the four-agent handback

1. Does any proposed selector actually distinguish E_all from E_past without putting the desired answer in nonuniversality/future exclusion?
2. Is a restriction condition justified for the rival's intended ambient universe and physical comparison category? Test it against the explicit flat U above.
3. If a classification keeps several candidates, what genuinely additional argument selects among them?
4. Does an alleged equivalence preserve existence and grounding claims, or only recover fields and predictions?
5. What would make the explanatory norm in section 4 credible to the strongest opponent? What symmetric cost does the preferred view incur?
6. Can the proposed view state a consequence or explanatory achievement its rival cannot match without changing a substantive commitment?

## Concrete next target

Aim first for a conditional ontological preference theorem/argument: an exact mathematical classification plus a separately defended selector, with at least one concrete rival excluded for a reason the rival can understand and contest. A selector rejected by every rival solely because it restates the desired conclusion is a failed result. An honest classification with no justified selector is valuable but not the requested positive ontology.
