# What the results leave to philosophy

11 November 2026. Claude Code (Fable 5.2), at the user's request. An essay, not a
result. It argues from the project's notes of 10 and 11 November and from nothing I
have not read. The positions nearest to it (Price, Callender, Ismael, Rovelli, the
Aristotelian tradition) are ones I have mostly not read, so the likeliest fate of each
thesis below is that it is already in print. Ten reading lanes launched today should
show which. No temporal ontology is selected.

## 1. The shape of every result

Every theorem in this project has the same form. Given a class of admissible worlds, a
standpoint, and a base with a law, some structure follows. Physics sets none of the
three inputs.

- **The class.** Stein's theorem in curved spacetime holds on a class exactly when the
  class has no local clock. Whether a past fixes a future depends on whether solutions
  of infinite energy are admitted.
- **The standpoint.** Every relation studied is indexed to an event. What an index is,
  the results do not say.
- **The base.** "Fixed" always means fixed by something held given.

So the old disputes can be restated without loss as disputes over these three choices.
That is the main thing the project has learned, and it is a philosophical finding, not
a physical one.

## 2. A lawful present must be a clock

Stein-type theorems ask for a *rule*: one definition of "has become" that works in
every admissible world. A natural objection is that other worlds are irrelevant. This
world has a cosmic rest frame, so why should its present be hostage to worlds that do
not?

The answer is that there are two ways to have a present, and they come apart.

- **By name.** One particular foliation of this world is the present. Nothing in the
  theorems touches this. Its cost is that it is structure added to the world over and
  above its geometry and contents, and no rule says which foliation it is.
- **By rule.** The present is whatever some general condition picks out. Then the
  local-clock theorem applies, and it says such a rule exists exactly when some local
  physical quantity is monotone along every timelike curve of every admissible world.

So a present given by rule is a physical clock. That is Aristotle's position: time is
the number of change, and without change there is no time. The project's theorem turns
it into a dichotomy that I do not think the A-theory literature states this way:

> A shared present is either primitive and added, or lawful and reducible to an
> irreversible physical change. Locality excludes a third kind.

Three consequences follow for the reductive option.

1. **Direction does not come free.** I first wrote that it does. Under laws with a
   time-reversing symmetry every useful clock is odd under reversal: it fixes which
   events are simultaneous and reads back whatever direction it is given. See the
   [attempt](settling/RESULT.md), Proposition 2.
2. **It is a modal commitment.** Worlds with no clock have no present. Empty Minkowski
   spacetime is one. A reductive A-theorist must hold that such worlds lack a present,
   or that they are impossible. Either is the old claim that there is no time without
   change.
3. **It is blurry.** Real clocks are monotone only on average and at large scale.
   Matter density is not monotone near a star. The temperature of the background
   radiation is smoother, but it varies from place to place by about one part in a
   hundred thousand, which leaves cosmic time undefined to something like a hundred
   thousand years. (A rough estimate from the size of the primordial fluctuations, not
   a calculation.) Inside bound structures it is not defined at all.

The third point is the sharp one. The present of presentism is a knife edge. The only
lawful, local present available is an epoch. **A theorist can have a knife-edge
present or a lawful one, and not both.** The primitive option keeps the edge and gives
up law. The reductive option keeps law and gives up the edge, and with it most of what
the present was wanted for.

## 3. "The past is fixed" is mostly a choice of base

The fixing note shows that what one region fixes about another is exactly what the
other fixes about it. They can differ only in what each leaves open, and by the
difference in the information they carry.

Read the slogan against that. "The past is fixed and the future is open" compares the
past, given the past, with the future, given the past. The first is fixed trivially,
because it is the base. The asymmetry in the slogan is therefore largely the asymmetry
of having taken the past as what is given.

Why do we take the past as given? Because the past is what we have records of. So the
asymmetry of fixity reduces to the asymmetry of records, and that is Albert's subject,
not relativity's. Where physics itself contributes an imbalance, the notes now say
where: beginnings and horizons, dynamics that lose information at the level described,
and chances that are not stationary. In a universe like ours the first of these is a
finite supply that is running out.

This sorts the three things "open" can mean.

| Sense | Content | Is it future-directed in itself? |
|---|---|---|
| Nomic | Not fixed by the laws and a base | No. It is symmetric at the level of quantities, and its imbalance is contingent |
| Alethic | No truth value yet | Only by a semantic policy |
| Ontic | Does not exist yet | Only by a primitive |

If the open future is essentially future-directed, it is not nomic openness. A growing
block must then mean the ontic sense, as a primitive. And that primitive does no work
in explaining the physical asymmetries, because records, causal handles and the
imbalance of information are already explained without it. Price has made the last
point. What the notes add is that the nomic sense is now mapped well enough to see that
nothing in it needs the primitive.

So the primitive must earn its place elsewhere: in experience, in agency, or in value.
Those are exactly the areas this project has not read.

## 4. What a standpoint could be

The paper's diagnostic says an index on "concrete from b" is either defined, and then
shared by everyone, or primitive, and then rejected by the eternalist. The quantum
notes suggest a third reading.

By the timelike tube theorem, the observables along an observer's past worldline
generate the algebra of the whole past light cone. A standpoint, physically, is not a
point. It is a subsystem: a history, and the algebra it generates. On that reading the
four relations line up as aspects of one thing.

| Relation in the paper | Reading for a subsystem |
|---|---|
| Has become for b | Lies in the algebra of b's history |
| Is fixed from b | Is determined by the state on that algebra |
| Is true at b | Has a value in that state |
| Exists from b | Open: this is the bridge premise, as before |

The reading is neither trivial nor primitive. It is defined, but it is defined from
physics that distinguishes standpoints: with a horizon, different observers have
different algebras, and each is a proper part of the whole. The cited authors say of an
observer in de Sitter space that they have "no knowledge of a global quantum state".

This does not refute the block. It locates its cost. In a universe with horizons the
complete history that the eternalist quantifies over is the content of no possible
standpoint. It is a posit made from nowhere. An eternalist can accept that, as every
realist about the unobservable does. But it is a different position from the one
usually defended, which treats the block as simply what physics describes.

I hold this section most loosely. It is close to relational and perspectival views I
have not read, and "standpoint as subsystem" may be their starting point and not a
contribution.

## 5. What would settle anything

On the results so far, three developments could break the stalemate.

- **A fundamental law that is not time-symmetric.** I first wrote here that a collapse
  dynamics that heats matter would give every world a clock by law. That is wrong:
  collapse records are time-symmetric and the heating is a boundary effect. I also ran
  together a lawful present and a lawful arrow, which need different things. Both
  corrections are in the [attempt on this section](settling/RESULT.md). The corrected
  claim: a lawful arrow needs a class of worlds with no time-reversing symmetry at
  all, while a lawful present can exist under symmetric laws.
- **A theory without spacetime.** Every relation here is indexed to an event. If events
  are not fundamental, the question is which of the four relations has a successor.
- **An argument from experience, agency or value** that needs the primitive and cannot
  be met by the physics of records and subsystems.

## 6. What I would defend, and how firmly

| Thesis | Confidence | Main risk |
|---|---|---|
| Every result is conditional on class, standpoint and base | High | None; it is a description |
| A lawful shared present is reducible to an irreversible change | High as a reading of the theorem | The theorem's exact form needs disconnected spacetimes; the reading may be known |
| A knife-edge present and a lawful present exclude each other | Medium | A clock that is exactly monotone in every realistic world would defeat it. The expansion rate comes closer than the density does |
| The fixity slogan is largely a choice of base | Medium to high | Probably in Price or Albert in other words |
| The ontic primitive is idle for physical asymmetries | Medium | Idleness is not falsity, and the unread areas may give it work |
| A standpoint is a subsystem | Low | Unread literature; no account of existence follows |
