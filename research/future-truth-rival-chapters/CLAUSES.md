## Chapter 7, "Bivalence, Future Contingents and the Open Future"

### Future contingents (p.100)

Paraphrase: Fφ and F¬φ (or metric Fnφ, Fn¬φ, with Fnφ entailing Fφ) whose
present truth-values "are not already settled by how things located in the
present or past of now are, or have been, in all their natural – non-future-directed,
non-Cambridge-like – respects." Such a statement's truth, if it is true, "would
anyway not be made inevitable, or be predetermined" by facts strictly about
the present or past. The authors presuppose that there are future contingents
(p.99).

### Lucretian properties are set aside, not refuted (p.101)

They say the debate about Lucretian (past/future-directed) properties is
undecided. "We here merely want to explore what follows from the assumption
that Lucretianism is of no avail in this context." Cambridge-like properties
of present things are rejected as grounds (pp.100–101). An appeal to causal
properties that nomologically necessitate the future is said to undermine the
statement's status as a future contingent (p.100).

### The semantic objection (pp.101–105)

1. Present grounding plus GBT (no future things) gives: neither Fφ nor F¬φ
   is true (p.102).
2. Inferring that they are *neither true nor false* needs ¬Fφ → F¬φ; this
   fails if time can end. Peircean F is rejected because it cannot express
   future contingents at all (p.102).
3. Kleene and Łukasiewicz gaps lose excluded middle; supervaluationism keeps
   classical theorems (p.104).
4. Supervaluationism is stated with forward-branching times and histories as
   maximal linear sets of *times*; Fφ is true at ⟨t,h⟩ iff some t*∈h, t≺t*,
   has φ true at ⟨t*,h⟩ (pp.104–105).
5. Objection to GBT: "if now is the last time, there can be only one history,
   that history having a last moment, namely now." So ¬Fφ & ¬F¬φ is supertrue
   and GBT would be committed to time having just ended (p.105).

### Deflationary grounding is noted, then set aside (p.106)

The Aristotelian schema ('p' is true, if it is true, because p), with
asymmetric 'because'. The authors "have sympathies" but concede that more is
needed and that "truth-grounding is always in terms of what there is and how
it is."

### Why present grounding is not mandated (pp.107–108)

- Content argument: on GBT, "prefixing an existential statement by a
  future-tense operator is a means to cancel any ontological commitment"
  (p.107). F∃xΦx and ∃xFΦx get different treatment.
- Parity with the past: Nero's still existing "is insufficient to ground the
  present truth of 'Nero was mad'" unless tropes or events are added. Absent
  those, GBT is no better placed for past truths than for future ones
  (pp.107–108).
- Distance analogy (p.108): why should it not be enough for a statement
  about the future to be true now "that its present truth will in the future
  be grounded … just as it is enough in order for a statement about what
  happens at a distance to be true here, that at a distance, its being true
  here is grounded by something's happening".

### The grounding principles (pp.108–110)

Note 3 (p.108) replaces Dummett's requirement with: "a proposition is true at
t only if sometimes there is something in virtue of which it is true at t."

With ↑ = 'one day hence', ↓ = 'one day ago', q = 'Some rain falls', and φ
restricted to statements "grounding-theoretically unproblematic and naturally
thought of as being in the simple present-tense" (p.109):

    (E1) Always, if φ is true, then ∃X(φ is true because X exist)
    (E2) Always, if φ is true, then ↓('↑φ' is true) because φ is true
    (E3) Always, if φ is true, then ∃X(↓('↑φ' is true) because X exist)

(E3) follows from (E1), (E2) and the transitivity of 'because'. The plural
quantifier ranges over "things, facts, tropes, events, etc." of one's
preferred ontology. Derivation: (1) '↑q' is true; (2) ↑('q' is true), by
Tarskian conditionals; (3) ↑∃X(↓('↑q' is true) because X exist); (4)–(5)
"One day hence, there will be things whose existence will explain why '↑q'
is true now" (p.109).

General requirement, with ψ ranging over "present truths of the problematic
kinds – including contingent truths about the future" (p.110):

    (GR) For all ψ, Sometimes, ∃X((Now, ψ is true) because X exist)

'Now' "always shifts the time of evaluation back to the time of utterance."
(GR) "allows for the possibility that a statement about the future be true
now whose present truth is sometimes in the future explained by what exists
then, while there exists now nothing that would explain its present truth"
(p.110).

### Future contingency kept compatible with present truth (p.110)

If a future contingent is defined as unentailed by all present truths, true
future contingents are trivially impossible. The authors' preferred
conception: present truths about the future, grounded by what will be,
qualify "as long as nothing there is or was, in conjunction with how it is or
was, makes it inevitable that, in the future, there will be such grounds."

### Negations and generalizations (p.111, n.5)

- ¬Fnφ: if time goes on at least n units, reduce it to Fn¬φ. Otherwise ¬Fnφ
  is true "because m time-units from the present, time has come to an end",
  where that is "a brute fact" (0≤m<n). "This grounding explanation
  presupposes that if time does not go on indefinitely, there is a last moment
  of time. This is not an entirely innocent assumption".
- ¬Fφ: if time goes on for every n, reduce it to ∀nFn¬φ. The grounding of
  generalizations is "a vexed issue" and "a problem for everyone." Otherwise,
  a last moment is again assumed, with a restricted generalization.
- Note 5: negative existentials were "conveniently glossed over by simply
  assuming that, in general, if F¬φ is presently true, sometimes in the future,
  its past truth will be grounded in whatever then grounds ¬φ."

### Symmetry with the past (p.112)

Under relaxed grounding, truths about past things "were, in the past,
grounded in how these things then were"; presentists can say the same. The
asymmetry they claim for GBT is not located in grounding.

### Indeterminism and openness (pp.112–116)

- Time remains linear: "there is no pressure to abandon the conception of time
  as being linear in favour of a conception of time as forward-branching"
  (p.112). Being determinate in truth-value is distinguished from being
  predetermined.
- (DET) Always, ∀tG∀t′(t≺t′ → the way the world is up to t nomologically
  determines the way the world is up to t′); (IND) is its negation (p.113).
- ω_t must exclude irreducibly future-tensed clauses (Fφ, Fnφ) and clauses
  reducible to ¬Fφ or ¬Fnφ, "lest (DET) be trivialised" (p.114).
- ω_t^loc: a complete description of entities *located* at or before t in
  natural respects, with "that's everything located" clauses. (DET_loc)
  Always, ∀tG∀t′(t≺t′ → □(At t, ω_t^loc → At t′, ω_t′^loc)), □ nomological.
  Permanentisms and GBT are all compatible with (DET_loc) (p.114).
- (IND_loc) grades (p.115): (I) the same things are located at t with
  different properties; (II) t and later times exist but contain no entity
  other than themselves; (III) neither t nor any later time ever exists. All
  three are said to be available to permanentism and GBT, except that (I) is
  unavailable to an eternalist ontology of events with essential
  non-spatiotemporal properties.
- ω_t^ex uses entities *existing* at or before t; (DET_ex), (IND_ex) and
  grades (I′)–(III′) are defined the same way (pp.115–116). (III′) is "no
  option" for permanentists. (II′) is very unlikely for them. GBT is
  consistent with all three. Openness as (IND_ex) of grade (II′) or (III′) is
  "perfectly compatible with the bivalence of all future-tensed statements"
  (p.116).
- Presentists can adopt the same openness only by making the past open in the
  same sense (p.116).

## Chapter 9, "Spatiotemporaryism"

### Setting (pp.135–139)

Spacetime points are the basic points of evaluation. The only objective
structure appealed to is "the fourfold division" of each point into itself,
its causal past, its causal future and its elsewhere region (p.135). The
causal future "includes the surface of the light cone … minus s" (p.137).

### Operators and axioms (pp.140–146)

Primitive: ▴ (everywhere in the causal past), ▾ (everywhere in the causal
future), ◂ (everywhere in the elsewhere region). Duals ▵, ▿, ◃.
⚫φ ≡ ▴φ & φ & ◂φ & ▾φ; ⚪ is its dual. Listed axioms: (A1R), (A2R), (A3R),
(A4R), (AR1), (A5R), (A6R), (AR2)–(AR4), (A7R) (transitivity), (AR5)
(symmetry of elsewhere), (A14R) ⚪▵⊤, and rules (R1R), (R2R), (RR).
⚫ and ⚪ "behave like ◽ and ⬨ in S5" (p.143). The @x operators follow
(A8R)–(A13R). (A16R) ⚪E!m. (A22R) ∀x(Sx → @xφ) → φ. (A9R) Sx → ⚫Sx.
(A23R) Sx → @xE!x. loc follows (A25R)–(A27R) and is not existence-entailing.
prec follows (A28R)–(A31R): irreflexive (the causality condition, citing
Hawking–Ellis p.190), transitive, and invariant across spacetime (p.145).
sep (D23) holds between distinct points where neither precedes the other.
Mixed axioms (A33R), (A34R), (AR6), (A24R) (pp.145–146).

### Ontologies (pp.146–150)

- (STAR) / (DYNR); (SPER) spatiopermanentism ⚫∀x⚫E!x; (STEMP) ⚪∃x⚪¬E!x.
- Spatiopresentism: (P2R) Sx → @x▴¬E!x and (P3R) Sx → @x▾¬E!x.
- Elsewhere options: (PO) Sx → @x◂¬E!x ("pointy"), (BO) Sx → @x◂E!x
  ("bow-tie").
- **Relativistic GBT**: (P1R) E!x → ▾E!x and (P2R) (pp.148–149).
- **Pointy relativistic GBT** = (P1R)+(P2R)+(PO). It is identified with Stein
  (1968): "those events, and only those, have already become … which occur at
  points in the topological closure of the past of a" (Stein, quoted p.149).
- **Bow-tie relativistic GBT** = (P1R)+(P2R)+(BO). "to the best of our
  knowledge … has never been defended in print"; it is "the best version of
  relativistic GBT" (p.150).

### Arguments for bow-tie over pointy (pp.151–156)

- Persistence-of-limbs objection (after Savitt 2000): on pointy views, the
  perceived limbs located in my causal past cannot still exist *and remain
  located* here-now. Bow-tie views avoid this (pp.151–152).
- Savitt's achronality requirement, recast as (3) E!x & E!y & Sx & Sy →
  ¬(x prec y), and Putnam's principle, recast as (4) E!x & @xE!y → E!y. On
  bow-tie views these are derivable only with (BO+) E!x → ◂E!x
  (pp.153–154).
- (BO+) collapses into (SPER) "as long as we limit the range of viable views"
  to the fourfold structure, and together with (C-AR2) it yields
  spatiopermanentism (pp.154–155). So bow-tie views reject (3), (4) and
  (BO+): "the intransitivity of being" (§9.4 title; conclusion pp.155–156).

### Recapture of the classical views (pp.156–161)

Bridge principles (PR1) x prec y & x sim z → z prec y and (PR2) @xφ & x sim
y → @yφ. They yield simultaneity classes, T*, ≺*, At*, and the totality
theorem (A32#). Translation and theoremhood are claimed with proofs in
Appendix 3, which was not read. Given the bridges, pointy views shrink space
to a point, "no suggestion in classical GBT either that the edge of becoming
is not really an edge but a point" (p.160). The authors "have not even begun
to negotiate between the two bow-tie versions" (p.161).

### What chapter 9 does not contain

Chapter 9's main text contains no truth-grounding principle, no relativistic
counterpart of (GR), no treatment of future contingents or bivalence, no
metric, and no field or determinism discussion. A metric tensor is said to
be needed only for dynamic spatiopermanentism's distance claims (p.151).
