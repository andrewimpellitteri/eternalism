# Referee report: "Horizons and Becoming" (research/horizons/main.tex, draft of 25 Oct 2026)

Reviewer role: hostile but fair, philosophy-of-physics journal. Read in full: main.tex, STRONGEST_VIEW.md, redteam/SYNTHESIS.md, PRIOR_WORK_CHECK.md. I did not read Geroch, Manchak, Goldblatt or any outside source; statements about them rest on PRIOR_WORK_CHECK.md. I re-checked the geometry of Props 2.5, 2.6, 4.4, 5.2 informally and found no error (details below); my objections are conceptual. Classification: MUST-FIX / SHOULD-FIX / OPTIONAL.

## Verdict

Major revision. Section 2 is a sound, honestly credited piece of causal-structure bookkeeping with a modest philosophical payoff. Section 4 is mostly a relabelling, and its one non-trivial observation (.2 fails for every spacetime with a finite conformal past, inflation or not) undercuts the claimed identification with the horizon problem. Section 5, the part meant to answer the "eternalism with tensed vocabulary" objection, does not succeed as written: the difference it exhibits comes from premises that the paper's own view rejects or cannot state, and it is a difference about the extent of spacetime, not about time.

## 1. Section 2 (settled vs real)

### 1.1 Is "settled" a good explication of the open future?
Partly. The paper defines Set(b) geometrically (every past-inextendible causal curve from x meets I^-(b)), then reads it as "fixed, under deterministic laws, by what is real from b". As an explication of "predetermined by what has happened" it is fine. As an explication of the open future it is not, for three reasons.

- MUST-FIX. Determinism vs settledness. The geometric condition is a property of (M, b); "settled" in the philosophical sense needs a property of the laws (deterministic, well-posed, domain-of-dependence property) *plus* the geometric condition. The paper says this in one sentence at the start of Section 2 and then drops it: the Remark ("the future closes", "the entire spacetime is settled") and the abstract speak as if the geometry alone settles things. In an indeterministic world (collapse dynamics, or any law lacking the DoD property) Set(b) settles nothing beyond I^-(b), whatever the geometry. The DoD premise is also the same locality premise as the view's own Bell falsifier (STRONGEST_VIEW s.8 item 1); say so. Repair: rename the geometric set (for example "determined from b", or "b-predictable", following Geroch's "domain of prediction") and reserve "settled" for the conjunction with a determinism hypothesis; state every result as "given laws with property DoD".
- MUST-FIX. Terminological collision. In the open-future literature "settled" is Belnap's (and Prior/Thomason's) historical-modal notion: no alternative possible histories through the present moment. That is not nomological determination from a region. A reader from that literature will take "settled from b" to mean something else. Belnap 1992 is read in the project (STRONGEST_VIEW s.7) but not cited in the paper. Cite it and say how the notions differ. (Under determinism they are connected; under indeterminism they come apart. The paper's notion cannot register contingency at all.)
- SHOULD-FIX. What "open from b" comes to. In a deterministic world, Set(b)'s complement is "not determined by b's fragment", which is a fact about b's information, not about the world's openness: every outside event x is determined by *its own* past. So the section's answer to Question 1 is "open-from-b" in a relational, informational sense. That is what the standpoint view can say, and it is worth saying, but it is exactly the sense an eternalist determinist can accept ("b's past does not fix x; the totality does"). The section should admit that an ontically open future needs indeterministic laws, and that Section 2 shows only that unreality of the future from b does not by itself imply (or exclude) its being determined from b. That is a useful dissociation (unreal but determined is the standard growing-block-plus-determinism position), but the paper presents it as new reading of "open".

### 1.2 Is the "closing at maximum expansion" remark significant?
It is an instructive curiosity with one real philosophical use, and the paper oversells it.
- What it is: for b after conformal time pi, I^-(b) contains a full Cauchy surface of the closed dust model, so the DoD argument covers all of M. This is the familiar fact that light can circumnavigate the closed space, combined with Geroch's observation that prediction beyond the past requires a compact slice (Manchak proved the refinement). The paper's own prior-work paragraph says as much. What is added is the sharp threshold and the phrase "closing of the future".
- Use: it is a clean example separating "not real from b" from "not determined from b" (the second can change with epoch while the first never does). That supports the claim that unreality does not entail openness. Keep it for that.
- Oversell: "Sharp closing of the future" suggests the world becomes closed in a metaphysical sense at a cosmic epoch. It is a nomological statement about a deterministic dust model with no inhomogeneities, no shell-crossing, no collapse, no quantum indeterminism, and it is false of our universe by observation (the paper's own Section 5 treats flat or sparse cases). The abstract states it flatly. Repair: phrase as "in the closed dust model with deterministic laws"; drop "closing of the future" from the proposition title or add "(nomological)".

### 1.3 Credit to Geroch and Manchak
Adequate, better than most drafts. The abstract says "essentially Geroch's domain of prediction"; Section 2 says "The notion is not new"; the relation-to-prior-work paragraph credits the Einstein-static case, Geroch's conjecture, and Manchak's proof and epistemic result. Remaining issues:
- SHOULD-FIX. "What is new here is the reading" and "We have not found ... the contrast between settled and real, in the sources read." The contrast between being determined-from and being real/realized is exactly Rakic's sea-fight issue (BJPS 48, 1997; project reading, STRONGEST_VIEW O14), and the unreal-but-determined future is a standing position in the growing-block literature. The paper does not cite Rakic at all. Cite her, and weaken "new reading" to "we apply Geroch's notion to the real/settled distinction".
- SHOULD-FIX. Earman (1995) and Hogarth (1993) are unread (PRIOR_WORK_CHECK). "We have not found the dust threshold" is properly hedged in the body; keep it out of any abstract-level novelty claim.
- SHOULD-FIX. Set(b) is defined with I^-(b), but the introduction adopts F(b)=J^-(b) under causal sufficiency, and STRONGEST_VIEW R4 says P6 gives J^-. The paper justifies the open set as "matching the light-cone freedom of the companion theorem". That is a defensible lower bound, but state plainly that Set is computed from the guaranteed part I^-(b) of the fragment; with F=J^- the set could gain boundary points and Theorem 2.2's proof (which needs an open complement-free compactness argument) would not go through as written (PRIOR_WORK_CHECK point 3 flags this).
- SHOULD-FIX (technical). Theorem 2.2 is stated for slices of one Cauchy temporal function t with range R, but Props 2.5, 2.6 and 5.2 apply it to conformal-time slices (eta = c), whose range is bounded. The "if" direction is fine for any Cauchy surface. The "only if" direction needs the proof to run along the eta-slices, which it does verbatim (the curve built leaves every compact set as eta goes to eta_-), but the statement should be made for an arbitrary Cauchy time function, or the equivalence "exists a Cauchy surface S to the past of x with J^-(x) meeting S inside I^-(b)" should be proved to be independent of the choice of slicing. As written there is a small gap in each application.
- OPTIONAL. Say the sets in the conformal examples are those of conformally flat models only; the general case is not "computable" in any sense stronger than Theorem 2.2.

## 2. Section 4 (standpoint logic and the horizon problem)

### 2.1 Is the modal reading of Box well motivated?
Conditionally.
- MUST-FIX. The paper never says what the atoms are or how A is evaluated "at x". If A is a tenseless claim about what occurs at x, then Box A is just "A held at every event in my causal past", i.e. the reflexive past modality H of Diodorean tense logic. Goldblatt's theorem is a theorem about that tense logic. The standpoint gloss ("from every standpoint real from b") adds nothing formal. The interesting reading would evaluate A *from* x (indexical or perspectival claims such as "p has happened"), and then persistence (what is real from x is real from later b: P5) is a constraint on valuations. With persistent valuations the natural logic is the intuitionistic-style logic of the poset along the future direction (monotone valuations), not the S4 of arbitrary valuations, and the directedness condition one gets is a condition on common futures, which Prop 4.4 shows holds. Whether I have this exactly right is for the author to check, but the point stands: the paper must state the object language, the valuation class, and whether persistence is imposed, because the horizon claim depends on it. As it stands "standpoint logic" is a label on the logic of the causal-past relation.
- MUST-FIX. Citations. The standpoint frame and its modal reading are Fine's and Lipman's (Lipman's standpoint logic, J. Phil. 2023, is the model STRONGEST_VIEW s.2b builds on). Neither is in the bibliography. The motivation for the reading, and for the claim that this is "the" frame, comes from them; without them the section looks like it invented the reading.
- SHOULD-FIX. Precision about the logic. "The logic is S4 but not S4.2" appears in the body and the conclusion. What is shown is that S4 is valid and .2 is not valid on these frames. That the logic is *exactly* S4 is left open by the paper's own Remark. Replace with "S4 is valid and .2 fails".
- SHOULD-FIX. The author list of the cited modal-logic paper is inconsistent: the text says "Lewis and van der Schaaf", the bibkey is vdSchaaf2026, and PRIOR_WORK_CHECK records it as van der Schaaf, arXiv:2601.14029v2. Verify the authorship before publication; I cannot.

### 2.2 Is identifying the horizon problem with failure of .2 illuminating?
Mostly a relabelling, and the paper's own results show why.
- By Sahlqvist correspondence .2 is directedness, and directedness of the past-generated subframe is, by definition, "any two events in J^-(b) have a common causal past". That the particle horizon is the absence of a common past is Rindler 1956. So Prop 4.2 plus 4.4 translates a textbook fact. The paper concedes this: the prior-work check says "the horizon problem is well known as 'no common past'". The identification claim is a one-line observation.
- Worse, Prop 4.4 shows .2 fails on the subframe of *every* event in every spacetime with finite conformal past, with or without inflation. The horizon problem and its inflationary resolution are quantitative: they concern the *observed* pairs. The modal property cannot distinguish standard cosmology (problem) from inflation (solved), as the paper admits when it says inflation restores .2 "for the observed pairs" only. So the thing the horizon problem is about is not the failure of .2 but the failure of a restricted directedness on a particular finite set of pairs, which is not a feature of the logic of the frame at all. "The cosmological horizon problem is this failure for our own standpoint" (abstract) is therefore wrong as stated: the failure holds on both sides of the problem/resolution divide.
- Related, SHOULD-FIX. Prop 4.4 is a statement about M. If a big-bang singularity or causal boundary is included as part of the frame (as one point or as boundary points), directedness may be restored or not depending on that choice. The result depends on treating the singularity as no standpoint; say so, and say what the modal logic does for spacetimes whose boundary is included. (Also: the proof extends to S^3 big-bang models, with the same argument; the proposition is stated only for R^3. OPTIONAL.)

What would make it illuminating: a statement the logic can actually express. Candidates: (i) a graded or restricted directedness (".2 holds on the subframe generated by the observable region"), which is a first-order property of the pair set; (ii) a philosophical reading of .2 in standpoint terms ("what is eventually always so from a standpoint is always eventually so from every later..." in the paper's direction), asking whether any metaphysical principle (agreement, convergence of fragments) corresponds to it. The paper offers neither. Absent them, demote Section 4 to a short remark or an appendix: "S4 holds; .2 holds in Minkowski (Goldblatt) and fails with a finite conformal past; this is the particle-horizon condition".

### 2.3 The "histories with no common origin" paragraph
Overreaches. MUST-FIX.
- "Standpoints whose own fragments are disjoint: histories with no common origin, coordinated only by having b in their common future." In a big-bang model both pasts approach the same initial singularity; they have a common *origin* in the singular boundary but no common *event* of M in the past. "No common origin" is therefore false in the usual cosmological sense, and "coordinated only by having b in their common future" is false as an explanatory claim: correlations in the data are explained, or not, by the laws and initial conditions, not by having a common future point. The sentence slides from a set-theoretic fact about two causal pasts into a causal-explanatory one (a Reichenbach-style common-cause thought) without argument.
- The fragments being disjoint is a fact about M's event set, nothing more. Repair: "their fragments, as subsets of M, are disjoint", and omit the explanatory gloss, or argue for it.

## 3. Section 5 (where grounding matters)

This section is the paper's main philosophical bid: to show that the standpoint-first view is not merely eternalism with tensed vocabulary. The "shadow" argument does not succeed as stated. What it establishes is narrower: **the equivalence with eternalism holds exactly when the existing standpoints' fragments cover M, and fails to be an equivalence when they do not.** That is a clean, honest observation, and the paper should say it in that form. It does not follow that this is a non-trivial disagreement about *time*.

### 3.1 (a) Restricting standpoints to matter events
- MUST-FIX. Dialectical problem with the view's own commitments. STRONGEST_VIEW O16 answers "why every event?" by arguing that worldline-only standpoints leave horizon regions uncovered (citing Earman 2008, s.6) and that with every event a standpoint, "the agreement of standpoints fixes the whole geometry". The view *officially* takes every event as a standpoint, and the companion theorem is stated for every event b. Section 5 then derives its difference from eternalism by adopting the variant (matter-only standpoints) that O16 rejects for this very reason. So the paper shows: on the official view, no difference (conceded); on a variant the view itself regards as worse, a difference. That does not help the official view. The paper's "Limits" paragraph calls the matter restriction "an extra premise" and moves on; it needs to confront the clash.
- Motivation. "A place where something happens" is not a matter restriction in any physical theory that has fields: the metric, the cosmological constant or vacuum energy, and quantum fields exist at every event of de Sitter space, so on a field ontology Gamma is all of M and the shadow is empty. The restriction therefore needs a notion of "matter" that is not fundamental and not geometric. It also drifts toward observer-relativity, which STRONGEST_VIEW O16 disclaims ("a standpoint is a location of possible becoming, not of consciousness"). Restricting to timelike worldlines excludes photons; including null worldlines changes the shadow. The choice of Gamma determines the shadow and nothing in the view fixes Gamma. As it stands the restriction is ad hoc.
- Compatibility with the companion theorem: logically compatible (the theorem classifies the *rule* F(b); which b exist is orthogonal), but the theorem's application to an ontology whose domain is G rather than M is not immediate. G=J^-(Gamma) is an open past set, not obviously globally hyperbolic, and the theorem is stated for globally hyperbolic spacetimes. SHOULD-FIX: say which spacetime the ontology's domain is and whether the theorem applies to it.

### 3.2 (b) Can the eternalist just agree?
Two separate things to say, and the paper should say both.
- The eternalist's position is not merely epistemic. She asserts that the shadow region exists and has a geometry and contents. That is a first-order existence claim, and the standpoint-first side denies it. So the disagreement is real, and "epistemic" is the wrong word for it.
- But it is a disagreement about the extent of spacetime, not about time. The structure is the old one between realism and a causal-access or verificationist constraint on existence: nothing in the shadow can signal to any matter, so by an Eleatic-type principle (to exist is to be able to affect something) it does not exist. An eternalist can adopt a restricted eternalism: "the block is G (or D) only". That eternalist agrees with the standpoint-first theorist on *which events exist* and differs only in grounding, which returns us to the original (possibly idle) dispute. So the shadow argument does not separate standpoint-first ontology from eternalism; it separates plenitude (maximal extension is real) from restriction. Which events the block contains is not a question about temporal ontology; the A/B dispute can be run on either extent.
- The strongest thing the standpoint-first side can say is that its restriction is *principled* (reality is whatever is real from some existing standpoint) while the restricted eternalist's is a physical hypothesis she needs a separate reason for. That is a fair point, but it moves the argument from the shadow to the principle "reality = union of fragments of existing standpoints", and that principle, not the shadow, is the contested premise. Say so.
- Note the symmetric idleness: the shadow region cannot affect any matter event, and no matter event can learn about it. The eternalist's shadow facts are as idle as the denial of them. The paper claims the underdetermination "is ontic" for the standpoint-first side; it is better described as non-existence of a region, not as underdetermination of its content.

### 3.3 Hidden global vantage and future-dependence of existence
These two points are, in my view, the most serious problems and the paper does not mention either. MUST-FIX.
- Global vantage. G, D and "shadow" are defined by quantifying over *all* s in Gamma across the whole of M, including standpoints to the future of and spacelike to any given b. From b's standpoint, those standpoints are not real, so "x lies in no J^-(s)" is not a statement that is true from any standpoint. Equivalently, the claim "there is a shadow" is made from the vantage of the totality, which STRONGEST_VIEW declines (P7 optional; O13 "the official view does not posit a standpoint of the whole"). The paper's own Prop 5.2 proves the shadow is nonempty by computing in the conformal model of the whole of global de Sitter space, i.e. by quantifying over the very events it says are not real. So either the claim is a statement in the eternalist metalanguage about a mathematical model (in which case "half the universe is real from no standpoint" describes the model, and the question of whether the model overrepresents reality is the disputed one), or it requires a standpoint of the whole. Repair: say explicitly which; the first is honest, and then the conclusion should be "the model has a region the ontology does not endorse", not "regions real from no standpoint".
- Future-dependence of existence. x is in G iff J^+(x) meets Gamma. On the matter-only variant, whether a past event exists depends on whether there is matter in its causal future. Add a particle in the shadow at late time and a region of the "past" comes into existence. For a view motivated by becoming (the past is what has become for later standpoints) this is odd: existence of x is hostage to the tenseless existence of later standpoints. It means the standpoint-first side is eternalist about standpoints (Gamma is given tenselessly) in order to differ from the eternalist about events. Say so and say how this squares with the view's treatment of passage (STRONGEST_VIEW O12).
- A more realistic version of this exists and the paper misses it. If all matter eventually decays (proton decay, black holes evaporating), then the matter-free future of the universe lies in no J^-(s) and is in the shadow: the standpoint-first-with-matter view says that time-after-the-last-matter has no facts. This is physically better motivated than a lone observer in de Sitter, and it connects to the old "time without change" and empty-time debates (Shoemaker 1969 is the usual reference; I am recalling it, not having checked it here). It also exposes the restriction as relationalism about time, a different thesis from the one the paper is defending.

### 3.4 (c) "No fact of the matter" vs agreement, and anti-realism
- Coherence with agreement (P5): coherent, trivially. Agreement concerns events real from more than one standpoint; the shadow is real from none.
- Anti-realism: the view disclaimed it, and Section 5 reintroduces a form of it. "No fact about the shadow" is not epistemic. It is an ontic restriction of existence to what is causally accessible to some matter, which is a verificationist or Eleatic constraint. The paper should name it as such and concede that the standpoint-first view, on this variant, is not only a thesis about grounding but a restriction on existence, with the usual costs: bivalence failure for statements about the shadow, or free-logic treatment of reference to shadow events. Say which. "Underdetermination" is the wrong word: underdetermination means several facts compatible with the data; here the claim is that there are no facts.
- Mixed categorical and entailed facts. D = G union Set(Gamma) includes events that are determined by the laws and real data but are real from no standpoint (Set(Gamma) minus G). Are there facts about them? If yes, then reality is not the domain of facts, and the identification of reality with the union of fragments gives way; if no, then determinism-given-DoD does nothing for the standpoint-first view and D should equal G. The paper's contrast ("the block says contents as definite as anything else; standpoint-first says no fact") mixes categorical facts with entailed ones. SHOULD-FIX: state the status of D minus G.
- The Limits paragraph undercuts the computability claim: it concedes that even D is fixed only given the spacetime (Manchak), not from grounded data alone. Then "its extent is computable" (What this shows) holds only relative to a given M, which is the eternalist's object. Another face of the global-vantage problem.

### 3.5 (d) Physical relevance of the lone observer
Low, and the paper concedes most of this, but the abstract does not.
- A single test geodesic in global de Sitter is an idealization (the paper says so). Realistic expanding universes (flat Lambda-CDM, etc.) contain matter at every comoving location, so every event has matter in its future and the shadow is empty by the paper's own argument. The physically interesting shadow cases are sparse universes and universes whose matter ends (Section 3.3 above).
- "Half the universe" is the proportion of area in the standard conformal diagram (the triangle I^-(Gamma) has area pi^2/2 in a pi-by-pi rectangle), not a physical measure. By spatial volume the shadow fraction tends to one at late times. Say what the measure is or drop "half".
- The observer-dependent horizon of Gibbons and Hawking is a bona fide physical notion (and is what the paper cites). The relevant physical point is observer complementarity (different observers' horizons differ). The paper does not use it, but it bears on the question whether the shadow is a feature of a given Gamma or of the world.

## 4. Overall assessment

Genuine contribution? Modest, and mostly outside the debate it is addressed to.
- Eternalism/presentism: Sections 2 and 4 are about causal structure and tense-logic on causal structure. Section 2 contributes a clean demonstration that the unreality of the future from b does not decide whether it is determined from b, with a computed example. Section 4 contributes a correspondence already implicit in the literature. Section 5 contributes the observation that the tenseless-equivalence objection to standpoint-first views has a precise domain of validity (coverage of M by fragments), which is useful, and an attempted escape from the objection that does not work.
- Single strongest claim: Section 2's separation of real-from-b and determined-from-b, with the exact geometric characterization and the closed-dust threshold. It is checkable, honestly credited, and relevant to the open-future literature.
- Single weakest claim: that the shadow gives the grounding claim "a precise locus" and that "there, and only there" the two ontologies disagree about what facts there are. The difference is produced by a matter-standpoint premise the view itself rejects, requires a global vantage the view disavows, is mimicked by a restricted eternalism, and concerns the extent of spacetime rather than time.
- What a referee would demand before acceptance:
  1. Re-state Section 2 with determinism explicitly hypothesized, the geometric set renamed or clearly distinguished from Belnap-settledness, and Rakic and Belnap cited.
  2. Specify the object language and valuations in Section 4, state whether persistence holds, and either demote the horizon identification to a remark or supply a statement the logic can express that separates the problem from its resolution.
  3. Rework Section 5: motivate Gamma non-adhocly or drop it; address the clash with O16 and the field-ontology objection; resolve the global-vantage and future-dependence problems (state it as a claim about the model, or accept the totality); confront restricted eternalism; state the status of D minus G; present the result as the coverage condition rather than as a rescue of the grounding thesis.
  4. Cite Fine and Lipman, correct the Earman attribution and the van der Schaaf author list.
  5. Remove "only there", "computable", "closing of the future" and "the horizon problem is this failure" as stated.

## 5. Overclaiming in abstract, introduction, conclusion

- Abstract, "the settled region is essentially Geroch's domain of prediction": fine, honest.
- Abstract, "nothing outside an event's causal past is settled ... everything is settled from every event after maximum expansion": MUST-FIX. Add "in the closed dust model, under deterministic laws with the domain-of-dependence property". As written it states a nomological result about an idealized model as a fact about the closing of the future.
- Abstract, "the cosmological horizon problem is this failure for our own standpoint": MUST-FIX (see 2.2): the failure holds with or without inflation.
- Abstract and Introduction, "There, and only there, an eternalist and a standpoint-first ontology disagree about what facts there are": MUST-FIX. "Only there" holds only for existence-of-events facts, relative to the translation thesis, and only if the matter-standpoint premise is granted. If "facts" include grounding or tensed facts the disagreement is everywhere (and is the conceded one). Restrict to "tenseless facts about which events exist".
- Abstract, "half the universe": SHOULD-FIX (see 3.5).
- Introduction, "The answers turn on horizons ... whether some regions lie in the past of no matter at all": acceptable framing, but see 3.1 on "matter".
- Section 5 "What this shows": "the point here is that for a standpoint-first ontology the underdetermination is ontic, and its extent is computable." SHOULD-FIX. Not underdetermination, and computable only relative to M.
- Conclusion: "The answers are computable from conformal structure." SHOULD-FIX: true for conformally flat (and conformal-to-strip) models only. "S4 without .2, with .2 restored by inflation on the observable domain": see 2.2 (the restoration is of a restricted directedness, not of .2 in the sense of Prop 4.2). "The grounding dispute has an empty shadow" in our idealized universe: acceptable, but it amounts to saying that in our idealized universe the paper's escape from the equivalence objection does not apply; state that directly, since it is the paper's own most important admission.
- The paper is otherwise careful in labelling what it has not found in the sources read. Keep that practice, but do not let "we have not found" statements stand in for novelty claims in the abstract.

## Checks I did on the mathematics (no objections beyond those listed)
- Cor. 2.4, Lemma 2.3, Prop 2.5, Prop 2.6 (dust threshold at conformal time pi when eta_b - c > pi): internally consistent; the S^3 bound in Prop 2.5 (eta_+ - eta_- <= pi) is used correctly; in Prop 2.6 for x with eta_x - c >= pi the closed ball is the whole sphere and cannot lie in an open ball of radius <= pi, so the "verbatim" argument holds.
- Prop 4.4: the criterion |X-Y| < eta_x + eta_y - 2 eta_- is correct for R^3; the no-common-past pairs at small c exist for every b. Generalizes to S^3.
- Prop 5.2: the union of I^-(s) over the pole geodesic is {d(X,N) < pi/2 - eta}; shadow nonempty; correct.
