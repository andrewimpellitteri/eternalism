# Novelty when everyone has the same frontier models

24 October 2026. Strategy note, not a result.

## The problem

If many people prompt the same models with the same literature, they will
converge on the same "obvious next" ideas. Our own path shows this: the
first ideas proposed here (the flat-space definability classification) turned
out to be a direct corollary of Madarász–Stannett–Székely 2025, i.e. exactly
the kind of result a model reaches quickly and so do others. Model-salient
ideas should be treated as already being worked on.

## The game

Expected payoff ≈ P(no one else publishes first) × value × P(it is correct).
Shared models raise the first factor's competition for salient ideas and lower
nobody's ability to produce plausible text. They do not make correctness or
verified depth cheaper. So the defensible edge is in the second and third
factors, plus speed on the first.

## Tactics

1. **Measure salience before investing.** Ask several fresh model instances,
   cold, "what is the open problem / next result here?" If they converge on our
   target, assume competition: either move fast or go deeper than they will.
   Stein-in-GR is moderately salient (Clifton–Hogarth name it as open); the
   energy-condition pocket problem and the causal-set contrast with
   Wüthrich–Callender are less so.
2. **Go where verification is long.** Models produce proof sketches easily and
   correct long proofs rarely. Results whose value lies in a checked chain of
   lemmas (gluing, domains of dependence, cone control) are harder to duplicate
   than one-line observations. Keep the independent cross-model review (Codex
   caught a real error here) as standard practice; it is part of the moat.
3. **Read primary sources at full depth.** Models summarize abstracts; the
   decisive facts here came from full texts (Clifton–Hogarth p. 385 open
   problem; Wüthrich–Callender's single-causet hypothesis; Brading 2017's true
   time; MSZ Theorem 5.1.2 killing our first idea). Unusual combinations of
   closely read sources are less likely to be rediscovered.
4. **Use the project's own assets.** Prior lanes (Brading/universe, causal-set
   growth, observer models) give combinations others will not start from, e.g.
   the same "locality across structures restores Stein" diagnosis in both the
   continuum and causal sets.
5. **Timestamp early.** Once a result is checked, a short arXiv/PhilSci preprint
   establishes priority cheaply; the full paper can follow.
6. **Prefer questions where a negative or structural result is also
   publishable.** The energy-condition branch already has publishable
   structural content (Obstructions 1–2) even if the full pocket problem
   stays open.
7. **Bring in a human expert early** for the parts models are weakest at:
   judging significance and knowing unpublished work in a small community.
8. **Be honest about what AI contributed** in any submission; it is becoming
   expected, and an accurate account is itself a differentiator.

## Limits

We cannot see others' private work. Searches (INSPIRE, Semantic Scholar,
arXiv) only cover what is public. Priority claims stay bounded to recorded
searches.
