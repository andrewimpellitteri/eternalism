# What the past can fix

*Exploratory later-chapter sample. It assumes earlier chapters have introduced
light cones and local becoming. Statements about this project's manuscripts
remain conditional and subject to their outstanding review gates.*

Imagine being allowed to inspect everything that has ever happened in your
past. Not just your memories, and not just the signals you have received:
every physical fact at every event from which a slower-than-light signal
could have reached your present location in spacetime. You also know the laws
and the geometry of the spacetime in which you live.

Could that information fix what happens next?

The gift is extravagant. No actual observer has such access. It is useful
because it strips away ordinary ignorance and leaves a more precise question.
We are asking what follows from a specified region of data, rather than what
a person can measure, remember or calculate. If even these questions come
apart, our ordinary word “prediction” needs to be handled carefully.

Determinism alone does not answer. A deterministic theory typically provides
uniqueness from suitable complete initial data. In relativity, an appropriate
complete surface crosses every possible causal history once: nothing can
arrive from an unrepresented direction without meeting it. The chronological
past of one event need not contain such a surface. Whether its data suffice
requires further investigation.

There is a useful geometric guarantee. Pick a target event and trace every
possible causal route backward from it, continuing each as far as spacetime
allows. Suppose every route enters the region whose data you possess. Under
suitable local deterministic equations, agreement on that region then forces
agreement at the target. No incoming causal route escapes the known region.

This kind of construction belongs to an established literature on domains
of dependence and prediction. Geroch developed the prediction question within
general relativity long before this project. Our companion manuscript studies
a particular geometric region and gives a way of recognizing its members
using successive complete time slices. The proposed criterion asks whether
one earlier slice cuts the target's entire causal past within the standpoint's
known chronological past. Its precise hypotheses and proof belong in the paper
and a technical appendix. A picture is an aid to that proof, not a replacement.

The word “guarantee” is doing essential work. If the geometric test succeeds,
the assumed laws fix the target's fields. If the test fails, we have not yet
learned that those fields can vary. An allowed route for influence is not
necessarily a route along which the chosen equations actually transmit
independently variable information.

An unusually simple law makes the distinction visible. Imagine that every
spatial location carries a quantity and its rate of change. The rate remains
constant; the quantity increases or decreases accordingly. There is no
communication between locations. The law is deterministic and local, and
nothing travels faster than light. It uses a specified family of timelike
worldlines, an additional structure we should acknowledge rather than disguise.

In ordinary flat spacetime, the entire chronological past of your event
includes some sufficiently early stretch of every one of those worldlines.
Knowing the quantity and its rate there fixes them at every later point on
that line. Repeating the reasoning at each location fixes the whole field
history. Yet many backward lightlike routes from a future target avoid your
chronological past. The geometric guarantee fails while this particular law
still supplies determination.

This example is not a model of all matter or a rival cosmology. It establishes
one logical point about the breadth of our assumption. A bound on propagation
speed does not force every dynamics to use the full range of permitted speeds.
If we want an exact account of what a particular theory fixes, we must examine
that theory's solutions, not only the surrounding light cones.

There are theories for which that further examination yields a sharp answer.
In the current manuscript, a conformally coupled scalar field on specified
spatially flat cosmological models provides an example. The proposed proof
constructs two smooth solutions agreeing throughout a standpoint's strict past
but differing at any chosen event outside its causal past. If the hypotheses
and construction withstand review, the boundary is exact for that solution
class. Notice what has made the stronger claim possible: an argument about
actual fields, added to the geometric argument.

Global geometry can also make the guarantee itself much stronger. Consider
an idealized closed universe whose space has the geometry of a three-sphere.
Light can travel around this space. If enough conformal time has elapsed,
the chronological past of an event contains an entire earlier complete spatial
slice. From data there, suitable deterministic equations fix fields throughout
the spacetime.

In the familiar closed dust model, the relevant strict threshold lies after
maximum expansion. This horizon fact is known cosmology, not a discovery of
this project. The significance for our argument comes from combining it with
the determination criterion and a separately stated account of becoming.
The model is idealized; neither its recollapse nor its matter assumptions
should be quietly attributed to our actual universe.

Now suppose, as a philosophical proposal, that what is real from an event
lies within its causal past and includes its chronological past. Future events
are then unreal from that standpoint. In the closed example, after the
threshold, the data on that chronological past nevertheless fix the fields
at future events under the assumed dynamics. An unreal future can be fixed
from what this interpretation counts as real.

The conclusion is conditional twice over. The physical equations must have
the required uniqueness property. And the interpretation connecting becoming
with a causal-past relation must be defended. The calculation does not establish
that interpretation. A determinist eternalist can accept the same calculation
while rejecting its vocabulary of standpoint-relative reality.

Our other manuscript approaches the interpretation from a different direction.
It asks what relations of becoming satisfy a particular package of requirements:
chronological becoming, transitivity, and one rule applied consistently across
a specified class of spacetimes under appropriate local embeddings. Its proposed
classification restricts nonuniversal relations to the region between the
chronological and causal past. That would constrain such theories, conditional
on those requirements. It would not prove that nature possesses a becoming
relation, nor show that every conceivable account of passage accepts the
requirements. In particular, demanding one compatible rule across many
spacetimes is stronger than asking for symmetry within a single universe.

There is a further temptation to resist. We might say that before the closed
universe reaches the geometric threshold, its future is open, and afterward
it closes. But that description would repeat the error we have just exposed.
Before the threshold, the geometry supplies a smaller guarantee; a particular
law may already fix more. The current scalar-field discussion even proposes
an antipodal echo: values can be related at separated events by special
properties of the equation and the closed spatial geometry. Such an effect
needs its own proof. It cannot be read from the guarantee's failure.

The distinction leaves a positive philosophical result intact. Denying that
future events are real does not by itself secure their freedom from determination
by earlier facts and laws. Whether that determination amounts to an objection
depends on what the defender wanted from an unreal future. Someone seeking a
distinction in existence may accept it. Someone hoping thereby to secure
alternative lawful continuations owes an additional argument.

An observer's knowledge remains another matter. We began by granting the
entire past, the laws and the global spacetime. Losing any of those gifts
changes the problem. Knowing that data would determine an event does not
mean an observer can collect the data, identify the correct model, compute
the consequence or verify the prediction. Geometry, dynamics, ontology and
knowledge meet here. Keeping their contributions distinct lets us see what
each argument has actually earned.
