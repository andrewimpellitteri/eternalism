# Tomorrow's appointment

*Exploratory opening chapter. The later chapters and technical appendices do
not yet exist. This sample tests an accessible argumentative voice.*

You have an appointment tomorrow. You know the place, the time, and the person
you expect to meet. You can already be anxious about it. You can prepare for
it, postpone it, or forget it. But the appointment has not happened.

What, exactly, is missing?

One answer is that the appointment does not yet exist. There are plans,
calendar entries and expectations, all of them present things. Tomorrow's
meeting itself is absent from reality. Another answer is that the meeting
exists at its own time, just as a distant city exists at its own location,
although you are not there. A third answer says that the past has accumulated
into reality, while tomorrow remains to be added.

These answers disagree about the extent of reality. They need not yet disagree
about what you will do. That is the first separation we need to make.

Suppose that the laws of nature, together with a complete physical description
of the world today, allow just one continuation. You will attend the appointment.
If that is how the laws work, a person could consistently believe that the
appointment does not yet exist while also believing its occurrence is fixed.
Absence from reality would not make the laws undecided. Conversely, someone
could believe that tomorrow's meeting belongs to reality while denying that
today's conditions and the laws necessitate it. On that account an actual
history includes the meeting, but the laws allow other histories too.

The last suggestion can sound like a trick. If there really is a meeting
tomorrow, how could it be otherwise? But consider what “could” is being asked
to mean. There is a difference between saying that a meeting occurs and saying
that it occurs in every history permitted by the laws with the same earlier
conditions. The second claim is much stronger. A description of what actually
happens does not, by itself, tell us the range of possibilities admitted by
the laws. An indeterministic account still has to explain actuality, chance
and alternatives. Merely asserting that future events exist does not discharge
that burden or settle it in the determinist's favor.

Now change the example. You are certain that you will attend, because you
never miss appointments. That certainty does not make the world deterministic.
It might be a reasonable judgment in a world whose fundamental laws allow
several continuations. And a deterministic world could contain people who
cannot predict whether they will arrive. They may lack information, face an
intractable calculation, or be unable to receive relevant signals in time.
The world's allowing one outcome and your being able to work out that outcome
are different matters.

We have separated three questions that ordinary talk often bundles together.
What exists? What do the laws fix? What can anyone know? None is dispensable,
and an answer to one cannot simply be carried over to the others.

There is also the feeling that tomorrow is approaching.

As the appointment draws near, anticipation may intensify. Afterward there
may be relief. A dated account of the meeting can remain correct throughout:
it takes place on a particular day at a particular hour. Yet “it is tomorrow,”
“it is now,” and “it is over” matter to you in different ways. Does this
changing significance disclose a changing feature of reality, or a changing
position within a reality whose events stand in fixed temporal relations?

It is tempting to settle the question by insisting that everyone experiences
time passing. But both sides must account for that experience. A defender of
objective passage asks whether descriptions in terms of dates and relations
leave something out. A critic asks what additional feature passage supplies
and how it can be stated clearly. Neither question disappears when we invoke
experience. We need to understand what each proposed explanation claims.

The familiar positions begin to come into focus. Presentism, in a standard
form, says that only present concrete things exist. Eternalism grants past
and future things a place in reality as well. Growing-block accounts include
the past and the present while excluding the future. These are families of
views, with substantial differences inside each family. They become interesting
when they meet difficulties rather than when they acquire a memorable name.

For the presentist, yesterday's promise is a useful difficulty. It still
obliges you, although the act of promising is no longer present. What makes
it true that the promise was made? A presentist may appeal to primitive facts
about what was the case, or offer another account of past truth. The point
is not that such a reply must fail, but that excluding the past from existence
does not remove our need to explain truths about it.

For the eternalist, your relief when the meeting is over raises a different
question. If the meeting has its place in reality regardless of when you
consider it, why does its being past matter so much? An eternalist can point
to your temporal location, memories, expectations and interests. The challenge
is whether that account explains everything at issue, including what the
defender of passage takes to be an objective difference. Calling the world
a block does not provide the explanation.

For the growing-block theorist, the difficulty is locating the growing edge.
If yesterday and its people still exist, yesterday's confident thought “this
is the present” belongs to reality too. What makes our confidence that we
are at the latest edge better placed? There are replies, but each must say
something substantive about presentness, consciousness or knowledge. Growth
is a proposal to examine, not a substitute for that examination.

Physics enters these discussions because an account of time must accommodate
what clocks, signals and experiments teach us. Relativity, in particular,
challenges the familiar idea of one universal simultaneity linking distant
events. But the step from a physical structure to a claim about existence
still has to be argued. A diagram can show which events can influence which
others. It cannot, without interpretation, tell us that influence is the
criterion of reality.

That step will matter when we reach local becoming: the proposal that what
has become is associated with an event or standpoint, rather than with one
present stretching across the universe. Mathematics can constrain such a
proposal once its requirements are explicit. Whether the constrained relation
deserves to be understood as becoming remains a further philosophical question.

The appointment has given us a way to approach that question without pretending
to answer it in advance. When someone says “the future is open,” we can now
ask whether they mean that future events do not exist, that the laws permit
different continuations, that no prediction is available, or that future-tense
claims lack a settled truth value. Those possibilities may be connected by
an argument. The connection should be shown.

Before choosing a theory of time, we need to know which question it answers.
Otherwise a discovery about clocks can quietly become a doctrine about
existence, and an intuition about freedom can quietly become a claim about
equations. The work begins by keeping those passages visible.
