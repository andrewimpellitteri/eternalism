# Completion, access, and becoming: a dilemma for causal-set growing-block readings

Sole-worker essay, 2026-10-21. This is a philosophical argument, not a new
physical result. It uses the project's mathematical and operational results as
constraints, and it keeps the mathematical, physical and bridge premises
separate.

## 1. The promise and the bill

Earman's 2008 assessment of the growing block is ambivalent in a useful way.
He argues that Broad's Becoming can be implemented in Newtonian models only by
"cheating": one starts with a block model, carves chips off it, and reassembles
them as a growing history. The resulting models are parasitic on the block in
two senses: they are constructed from block chips, and a condition of adequacy
is that they yield the block back as an "ideal completion" of Becoming
(Earman 2008, pp. 135-136, 158-160). Yet Earman also says the causal-set
programme "avoids any appearance of parasitism on block models" because
spacetime is supposed to emerge from the growth, not the other way round
(pp. 159-160). That is the promise: becoming would be primary, and the block
would be a derived description.

The promise has a bill. A growth law is a probability (or amplitude) assignment
on finite histories. To speak of the completed history, or of a global fact
about what has become, one needs an extension of that assignment to the
infinite history space. For classical sequential growth this is usually
available. For complex percolation outside the real segment it is not: on the
sigma-algebra generated by naturally labelled doubly-principal cylinders, no
finite countably additive complex measure matches the prescribed amplitudes
when `p` is outside `[0,1]` (`research/paper-novel/main.tex`, Theorem 1;
variation bound `V_n >= |p|(|p|+|1-p|)^{n-2} c_s`). The obstruction is not a
failure of one clever construction; it is a finite-total-variation obstruction
on a fairly small event algebra.

This paper asks what that theorem does to the growing-block reading. The
answer is not "it refutes becoming." It is that the theorem turns Earman's
"ideal completion" into a conditional and exposes a dilemma: a reading that
wants a global completed history must either restrict itself to model classes
where the extension exists, or accept that its completion is a block limit;
a reading that retreats to finite or accessible events can keep becoming, but
it gives up the global completed-history claim that the growing-block picture
usually advertises. The choice between these is a bridge premise, not a
consequence of the mathematics.

## 2. What the mathematics actually says

Three qualifications matter for the philosophy.

First, the theorem is about **complex percolation**, a one-parameter family of
growth laws with complex transition amplitudes. It is not a theorem about every
causal-set dynamics. Classical real percolation and the Dirac endpoints extend;
the failure is specific to the non-real parameter regime. A philosopher should
not generalise "complex percolation outside [0,1] has no global scalar measure"
to "causal-set growth cannot ground becoming."

Second, the theorem is about a **scalar complex measure** matching the
prescribed cylinder values. It does not say that no measure of any kind exists
on the covariant event algebra. A Dirac measure at a completed causet is a
countably additive probability measure, but it generally does not encode the
growth amplitudes; a norm-only Hilbert-valued realization can match cylinder
norms while changing interference (`main.tex`, scope section). The obstruction
is to a specific completion demand: preserve the scalar amplitude rule and
extend it countably additively.

Third, the theorem is **conditional on the event algebra**. The
doubly-principal algebra is smaller than the full cylinder algebra, and the
failure on the larger algebra does not automatically transfer to it; that is
exactly why the smaller case was open. The theorem closes that gap for the
scalar rule, but not for every possible restriction, summation method or
quantum measure.

With those qualifications, the philosophical constraint is precise:

> If a reading of complex percolation requires a global completed history whose
> event probabilities are the prescribed scalar amplitudes, then for
> `p` outside `[0,1]` that reading has no such history to be about.

That is a conditional, but it is not trivial. It says the "ideal completion"
Earman treats as a condition of adequacy may fail to exist in a model class
that was supposed to illustrate becoming.

## 3. Three ways to read the growth

It helps to distinguish three readings, because they incur different bills.

**(A) Completed-history realism.** The growth is a process toward an infinite
completed causet; the infinite history is the real object, and finite stages
are approximations to it. This is the reading that most naturally supports a
global growing block: there is a fact about the whole history, and it grows.
It is also the reading that needs the extension theorem. For complex
percolation outside `[0,1]`, (A) is excluded under the scalar amplitude rule.

**(B) Block completion.** The infinite block is the ideal completion of the
finite growth; the growth is a way of presenting the block, not a fundamental
becoming. This is Earman's "parasitism" horn. It is not a refutation of the
growth story; it is a claim about what the growth story is a story of. The
block is prior in the order of explanation, even if the growth is
epistemically or computationally prior.

**(C) Local/functional becoming.** There is becoming at finite stages, in
local regions, or relative to a system, but no unique global completed history
with a single fact of the matter about "what has become" simpliciter. This is
the reading suggested by Wüthrich and Callender's gauge-invariant growth and
by Brading's system-relative present. It can survive the non-extension theorem
because it does not demand the global scalar extension.

The dilemma is between (A) and (B)/(C). The theorem does not choose between
them. It does, however, make the cost of (A) explicit, and it makes the
difference between (B) and (C) philosophically load-bearing.

## 4. The dilemma

**Horn 1: keep global completion.** Then, for complex percolation outside
`[0,1]`, the scalar amplitude rule cannot be extended. The reading must either
restrict the model class (e.g. to real percolation), weaken the amplitude rule,
or accept a different event algebra. Each is a substantive revision, not a
free move. If the reading insists on the original rule and the global history,
it is inconsistent with the theorem.

**Horn 2: give up global completion.** Then the reading can keep finite
additivity or a local event algebra, but it loses the claim that there is a
single completed history about which global becoming facts are true. This is
not a small concession: it is the difference between "the sum total of
existence is increasing" (Broad's phrase) and "there is a local, system-relative
or stage-relative becoming." The former is the growing-block picture; the
latter is a family of weaker views.

Earman's "ideal completion" is the pivot. If the block is only an ideal
completion that need not exist as a measure-theoretic object, then Horn 2 is
compatible with a deflationary reading of the block. If the block is a
condition of adequacy, then Horn 1 is mandatory and the non-extension theorem
is a genuine exclusion for the model class.

The philosophical question is therefore not "does the theorem prove
eternalism?" It does not. The question is "what does the growing-block reading
claim about the completion?" The theorem constrains the answer but does not
supply it.

## 5. The strongest objection: finite becoming is enough

The best objection is that the dilemma is rigged. Why should becoming require a
global completed history at all? Wüthrich and Callender argue that causal-set
growth can support a gauge-invariant growth in the number of elements even when
the identities of elements are indeterminate, and they preserve a qualified,
"exotic" becoming at a metaphysical cost (arXiv:1502.00018v1, Secs. 4-5). On
that view, the failure of a global scalar extension is beside the point: the
becoming is in the finite growth, not in a completed object.

Brading's presentism makes a stronger version of the objection. Her present is
not a global simultaneity slice; it is the spatiotemporal region of whatever
size is needed to sustain a dynamical system's law-constituted unity
(*Presentism as an Empirical Hypothesis*, 2013; *Physically Locating the
Present*, 2015, pp. 13-19). If the present is system-relative, then the demand
for one global completed history is a demand from a different, block-minded
picture. The non-extension theorem may constrain that picture without touching
hers.

This objection has real force. It shows that Horn 2 is not a defeat; it is a
substantive rival. It also explains why the mathematical result should not be
advertised as an argument for eternalism: the local/functional reading can
accept the theorem as a constraint on a global ambition it never had.

## 6. Reply: what the objection concedes

The objection succeeds against a strong reading of the dilemma, but it
concedes the central point. A local/functional becoming is not the same thesis
as the growing block. It gives up:

1. **A unique global fact of becoming.** There may be no single answer to
   "which events have become?" beyond a stage or a system.
2. **A global truth about the future's non-existence.** The growing-block
   slogan "the future is literally unreal" is hard to sustain if there is no
   global completed history to contrast with the present.
3. **A completion-independent identity of the actual history.** Wüthrich and
   Callender's gauge-invariant growth explicitly leaves the identities of
   elements indeterminate; Earman notes the resulting indeterminacy of
   Becoming for non-unit equivalence classes (Earman 2008, p. 158).

These are not refutations. They are costs. The philosophical gain of the
dilemma is that it forces the costs to be stated. A reading that accepts them
is coherent; a reading that denies them while also denying global completion is
not.

There is also a mathematical echo of the access problem. The event-filter work
shows that the divergent global variation does not automatically become an
accessible detector probability: in the finite model, the coherent filter's
success probability approaches `1/4` while the event measure diverges
(`research/event-filter-resources/RESULT.md`). The covariant-observer
comparison shows that a cardinality-only algebra cannot resolve the
phase-aligned event, while an unlabelled-class quotient can in the symmetric
models (`research/covariant-observer-witness/RESULT.md`). These are model
results, not a general theory of access, but they support the philosophical
point: "global becoming" and "accessible becoming" are different notions, and
the non-extension theorem bears directly only on the former.

## 7. Predecessors and what is new here

- **Arageorgis 2016** argues that the growing-block imagery the causal-set
  programme pursues is "parasitic on a block universe view" because the
  dynamics is a measure defined only in the infinite-time completion
  (*Belgrade Philosophical Annual* XXIX, p. 52). The non-extension theorem
  sharpens this: for a model class, the completion may not exist at all. But
  Arageorgis's criticism is about the *need* for completion, not about the
  measure-theoretic existence of one; the theorem adds a conditional premise.
- **Earman 2008** supplies the "ideal completion" and parasitism framing and
  the causal-set promise of a non-parasitic growth. The theorem shows that the
  promise is model-dependent: the ideal completion can fail.
- **Wüthrich–Callender 2017** supply the strongest escape: gauge-invariant
  growth with indeterminate identities and a qualified becoming at metaphysical
  cost. The dilemma accommodates their view as Horn 2/C, not as a refutation.
- **Brading 2013/2015** supplies the system-relative present that makes the
  global demand optional. Her view is the most developed alternative to the
  dilemma's Horn 1.
- **Stein 1991** supplies an event-relative notion of definiteness: the
  printed theorem (p. 149) characterizes a relation of "already definite as
  of" a point, with the contextual condition that some states remain unsettled.
  Stein's local relation is not a global becoming; it is a relativistically
  respectable local structure. The theorem's literal display omits condition
  (iv), which the proof uses; the article cites it with that premise.

The formulation of the dilemma is, I think, the new philosophical contribution
here. The ingredients are known: Arageorgis's parasitism charge, Earman's
ideal completion, Wüthrich–Callender's gauge growth, Brading's system-relative
present, Stein's event-relative definiteness. What is new is the explicit
claim that the non-extension theorem turns Earman's ideal completion into a
conditional and that the escape to local/functional becoming is a substantive
thesis with listed costs. This is not a new ontology, and it is not a proof of
eternalism.

## 8. Verdict

**Known in parts, new as a precise dilemma; the defence is conditional, not a
refutation.**

The strongest argument for the dilemma is that it respects the mathematics:
the theorem is a real obstruction to one completion strategy, and it would be
dishonest to call the growing-block reading unaffected while it relies on that
strategy. The strongest argument against it is that the growing-block reading
need not rely on it: local/functional becoming, gauge-invariant growth and
system-relative presents are live alternatives. The decisive consideration is
therefore not the theorem alone but the reading's own completion claim.

If the reading claims a unique global completed history with the prescribed
scalar amplitudes, the theorem excludes it for complex percolation outside
`[0,1]`. If the reading claims only local or system-relative becoming, the
theorem constrains a global ambition it does not have, and the remaining
question is whether the weaker thesis deserves the name "growing block." That
is a philosophical question, not a mathematical one.

## 9. What would change the verdict

- A global countably additive measure for complex percolation outside `[0,1]`
  on a smaller physical event algebra, with the scalar values retained, would
  remove Horn 1's exclusion.
- A derivation of global becoming from a local/functional account, without
  invoking a completed history, would collapse the dilemma into Horn 2 and
  make the growing-block label defensible.
- A physical observer model showing that the divergent global event is
  accessible at finite cost would make the access objection to Horn 2
  concrete; the present event-filter results are model-specific and do not
  establish this.
- A demonstration that the growing-block reading can do all its explanatory
  work without any completion claim would make the theorem irrelevant to it,
  and the dilemma would be a non-issue rather than a constraint.

## 10. Limits

This essay is a philosophical argument, not a primary-source-complete history.
Earman 2008 was read in the supplied PDF at the abstract, Sections 2-8 and the
conclusion; Arageorgis 2016 at the conclusion (printed p. 52); Wüthrich–
Callender at the arXiv v1 Sections 4-5; Brading through the project's
full-text source records and published-layout copies; Stein 1991 at the
printed theorem and surrounding pages. It does not settle the metaphysics of
the growing block, and it does not claim that any of these authors would accept
the dilemma as formulated. The mathematical claims are the project's scoped
results, not new theorems.

## 11. Coda: the exclusivity premise

The companion audit (`research/discriminators-audit/RESULT.md`) screened 21
candidate tests and found none that qualifies as an ontology discriminator
under the manuscript's standard. That result can look like an empirical failure:
after all the detectors, no experiment separates the views. It is better read as
a structural lesson.

A detector reading selects a physical model, not an ontology. A foliation, a
stochastic collapse noise, a discrete order, or a cosmological arrow can be
carried by a block package as well as by a presentist or growing-block package.
The audit's D1 (LAGEOS / Einstein-aether) is the clearest case: a nonzero
preferred-frame parameter would select a gravity model, and a block universe
with the same aether field is admissible. The missing premise is not a better
measurement; it is an **exclusivity premise**: a defended claim that one
ontology's constitutive commitments entail a structure with a specific coupling,
and that the rival's commitments forbid it. No candidate in the screened
literature supplies that premise.

This is the same lesson as the completion dilemma. The mathematics constrains
which models can carry a global completed history; it does not tell us which
ontology owns the model. The audit's negative result therefore shifts the
burden rather than ending the debate: the next move is a philosophical defense
of an exclusivity premise, or an argument that the ontology labels are doing no
independent work.

The strongest objection is that no extra premise is needed because an ontology
just is the structure of its best model. If so, the debate collapses into a
structural-realist reading and the labels "presentism" and "eternalism" lose
their distinctive content. The reply is that this is a substantive metaphysical
position, not a neutral dissolution; it should be argued for, not assumed. The
audit's conclusion is thus conditional in the same way as the essay's: given a
non-structural reading of the labels, an exclusivity premise is required, and no
detector supplies it.

That is a philosophical result, not a failure of experimental imagination. It
tells us where to look next: at the constitutive commitments of the views, not
only at their detectors.
