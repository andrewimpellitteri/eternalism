# Grounds in the Briggs–Forbes law-based growing block

**Scope.** Bounded audit of the primary open article *The future, and what might have been* (published online 30 January 2018; *Philosophical Studies* 176 (2019), 505–532). The publisher HTML was read selectively at §§1, 1.1, 2, 2.1 and 2.2. This is a selected full-text reading, not an audit of the entire paper or of the 2012 chapter on which it builds.

## Grounds contract stated by the authors

- **Ontology and passage:** The view says past and present things and events exist, future things and events do not, and passage is new things coming into existence. The authors explicitly say they do not defend the view in this article (§1, publisher HTML; pp. 506–507). This is a growing-block thesis, not a claim that the future already exists.
- **Representation and future truth:** The concrete actual block is represented by abstract, static timelines. Tensed propositions are evaluated relative to a time and timeline (§1.1). The authors summarize the earlier Briggs–Forbes account: future truths are made true by past and present things plus laws (§1.1, citing Briggs and Forbes 2012, p. 298). Feasible extensions share the actualized timeline as an initial segment; histories are maximally specific feasible ways it could turn out. With their adopted supervaluation, a sentence is true at a time in a timeline if true in every feasible-history extension, false if false in every such extension, and otherwise indeterminate (§1.1).
- **Laws and grounds of laws:** Laws are timeless sentences represented in each ersatz timeline, while tensed propositions take values at times (§2.1). A timeline's laws must be logically closed and consistent with its represented sequence (§2.1). For the concrete-world side, ordinary tensed truthmakers are concrete things at times. Candidate *lawmakers* are expressly left open: dispositions, Aristotelian natures, capacities, structural-equation facts, or unnamed reality-making features. Whatever they are, the authors require them to constrain how the block grows (§2.1). They say their account places “very few constraints” on lawmakers.
- **Feasibility:** A feasible timeline shares both the history and laws of the actual timeline (§2.2). The authors say laws and past are fixed while the future is open; feasible timelines represent possibilities that might become actual as the block grows (§2.2). This is their intended modal structure, not a claim that physics independently establishes it.

## Strongest test: two same-past, same-law continuations

Take a present timeline (T) with two feasible history extensions, one in which a specified future event (Q) occurs and one in which it does not. This is exactly the structure described in §1.1: histories are feasible extensions of the timeline, and the truth clause quantifies over all of them. By §2.2, the extensions share (T)'s history and laws. Therefore, on the authors' selected supervaluational semantics, (Q) is indeterminate at (T): not true in every feasible history and not false in every feasible history. The disjunction (Q\lor\neg Q) remains true in each classical history and is supertrue at (T).

This is the decisive result for the preliminary three-premise obstruction. The Briggs–Forbes framework does **not** furnish two opposite, outcome-matching bivalent truth values *at the shared present standpoint* for this unsettled (Q). It rejects preliminary premise **C** for such cases: the current value is not stipulated to match whichever future occurs in a designated complete model. It retains a semantics in which some future propositions are true or false now when all feasible histories agree, and others are indeterminate. Thus the test does not refute their account by deriving a contradiction from A, B, and C; it declines C's bivalence condition for the chosen proposition.

Does the same classical causal past imply the same complete grounds here? **No, not from the stated account.** Sharing history in the feasible-extension relation holds the past and laws fixed, but the authors do not identify complete grounds with the classical field restriction to a causal past. They posit further lawmakers, leave their nature open, and require only that they constrain growth. Nor do they specify a relativistic causal-past domain or prove that equal classical past data entail equal lawmakers. Within one feasible family, the same past and same laws can support multiple open continuations; under supervaluation this yields indeterminacy rather than two different bivalent present truths. Across distinct concrete worlds, equality of the classical past alone does not establish equality of primitive lawmakers or complete grounds.

This answer is importantly weaker than the preliminary B premise. The paper does not demonstrate that lawmakers vary while the entire past is held fixed, and it does not give a field-theoretic counterexample. It simply leaves the reduction of lawmakers to classical past data unspecified. So same-past/same-complete-ground identity is neither established nor disproved as a general metaphysical thesis by this source.

## Assessment and limits

This is a strong law-based growing-block reply to the generic demand for future truthmakers: a future event need not exist for a present/past concrete base plus primitive laws/lawmakers to support future truth, while open feasible extensions permit present indeterminacy. The view is not a generic refutation of eternalism or of the preliminary semantic baseline. It offers a different truth-value policy and additional primitive modal structure.

The source's own commitments expose the remaining pressure point: “laws” are not merely equations derived from a classical past state in the account as presented. Their truth is grounded by lawmakers, whose candidates and cross-world identity conditions remain open. Replacing those grounds with the preliminary (s|P_b) data would add a philosophical/physical premise not supplied by the paper. Conversely, treating lawmakers as extra facts means the same-past test must compare those facts too before it can instantiate preliminary B.

The strongest result is semantic and conditional on the authors' model: same history and laws plus divergent feasible futures make the relevant future sentence indeterminate at the shared timeline. It does not establish that actual physical laws permit those divergent histories in any specified relativistic field theory, that all future claims are indeterminate, or that primitive lawmakers are a satisfactory explanation. The article expressly declines a defense of growing block and does not offer a complete theory of the nomological package (§§1, 2).

The 2012 chapter is not needed for this bounded test. Its publisher abstract was the only checked version in the preliminary register; this audit does not conflate that chapter with the 2019 article's summary or silently substitute a draft.
