Source IDs are in sources.jsonl (prefix `hot-`).

## 1. Main positions

- **Aristotle: time is the number of change.** Time is neither change nor independent of it; it is
  change as countable by before and after, and the "now" plays the part of the unit. Rest is measured
  indirectly, since what rests could move. Aristotle also says there is one time everywhere at once.
  *Physics* IV.10–13, Hardie and Gaye (`hot-aristotle-physics-iv`, full-text; ch. 14 not on the copy
  read). Whether this is a thesis about existence or only about awareness is disputed (Coope 2001, a
  lead only).
- **Augustine: only the present exists, and time is measured in the mind.** Past and future are not;
  there is a present of things past (memory), of things present (attention) and of things future
  (expectation). Time is not the motion of a body, since we also measure rest by it. God made time
  and stands outside it. *Confessions* XI, Pilkington (`hot-augustine-confessions-xi`, full-text,
  selected sections).
- **Newton: absolute time.** True time flows equably without regard to anything external and is
  called duration. Sensible measures by motion are relative time. There may be no perfectly equable
  motion, and duration stays the same "whether the motions are swift or slow, or none at all". *Principia*,
  Scholium to the Definitions, Motte (`hot-newton-principia-scholium`, full-text).
- **Leibniz: time is an order of successions.** Times apart from the events in them are nothing.
  Two "worlds" differing only by a shift in time are one world, by sufficient reason and the identity
  of indiscernibles. Clarke, for Newton, answers that sufficient reason may be God's will, and that
  space and time are quantities while order is not. Leibniz–Clarke correspondence, Bennett's
  modernised abridgement (`hot-leibniz-clarke-bennett`, full-text, selected). Not the original wording.
- **Kant: time is a pure form of inner intuition.** It is not empirical, it is given a priori, and one
  cannot think it away from appearances though one can think appearances away from it. It is empirically
  real and transcendentally ideal. *Critique of Pure Reason*, Transcendental Aesthetic §§5–7, Meiklejohn
  (`hot-kant-cpr-aesthetic`, full-text).
- **McTaggart: time is unreal.** Time needs change, change needs the A series (past, present, future),
  and the A series is contradictory. "Time", *Mind* 17 (1908) (`hot-mctaggart-1908`, full-text, selected).
  The tense-and-language lane covers the later debate.
- **Bergson: lived duration against clock time.** He praised relativity as physics but held that the
  physicist's many local times do not exhaust time. Einstein replied in Paris, 6 April 1922, that there
  is no philosopher's time apart from a psychological one (reported, not read in the original). Bergson,
  *Durée et simultanéité* (1922) was **not accessed**. Known only from SEP Bergson (**secondary**,
  `hot-sep-bergson`, discovery) and from Unnikrishnan (`hot-unnikrishnan-2020`, abstract and excerpts;
  a dissenting physicist's reading).
- **Buddhist momentariness.** Everything conditioned arises and perishes in an indivisible moment, and
  apparent persistence is a causally linked stream. Abhidharma schools (Sarvāstivāda, Theravāda) built it;
  Dharmakīrti's tradition argues for it from causal efficacy: existing is being able to produce an effect,
  and nothing persistent could do so at some times and not others. Sources: SEP Abhidharma §3
  (`hot-sep-abhidharma`) and SEP Ratnakīrti §2 (`hot-sep-ratnakirti`), both **secondary**, full-text of those
  sections. No primary Buddhist text was read. Nyāya replies (Udayana) are named in SEP but their content was
  not read.
- **Shoemaker: time without change is possible.** A world of three regions, each freezing for a year
  on a pattern that makes a total freeze the best inductive explanation, would give its inhabitants
  reason to believe in changeless intervals. "Time without Change", *J. Phil.* 66 (1969) 363–381
  (`hot-shoemaker-1969`, full-text, selected pages 367–380; copy read was a third-party course upload of the
  journal scan). Relationist rejoinder: Morganti 2017 says relationism can be stated without change
  (`hot-morganti-2017`, abstract only).
- **Physical-clock relationism, the modern descendant.** Rugh and Zinkernagel say time has a "physical
  basis" only if some actual or possible physical process can occur in it, an Aristotelian and Leibnizian
  view that allows counterfactual clocks and is not reductive (`hot-rugh-zinkernagel-2008`, abstract,
  intro and §2.2). Gryb and Thébault keep succession absolute and duration relative (`hot-gryb-thebault-2014`,
  abstract and two passages).

## 2. Canonical arguments

**A. Aristotle: no time without change** (*Phys.* IV.11). (1) We register time only when we mark a before
and an after in some change. (2) When the mind notices no change, as with the legendary Sardinian sleepers,
no time seems to have passed. (3) Before and after belong first to magnitude, then to motion, then to time,
and time goes with motion. (4) So time is not independent of motion, and is the number of motion by before
and after. *Objection:* (2) is about awareness, and the step to existence is the gap. Shoemaker (pp. 367–368)
grants that nobody can know during a changeless interval that it is one, and denies that this shows such
intervals impossible. Aristotle's reply would need (3), which is asserted, not argued.

**B. Leibniz: the shift argument** (third and fourth papers). (1) Nothing is so without sufficient reason. (2)
If time were a thing distinct from events, God could have made the world a year sooner, with no reason to
prefer either date. (3) So time apart from events is nothing, and the two "worlds" are one. *Objection (Clarke):*
the sufficient reason can be God's will, which Leibniz says amounts to denying the principle; and time is a
quantity while order is not. (Modern replies of sophisticated substantivalism were not read.)

**C. Shoemaker: changeless intervals are conceivable and knowable.** (1) Three regions A, B, C freeze for a year
on cycles of 3, 4 and 5 years (p. 370ff). (2) Inhabitants observe each local freeze from the unfrozen
regions. (3) Two hypotheses fit the data. One says total freezes occur every 60th year, the other says all three
skip it every 59 years. The first is simpler (p. 373). (4) Unverifiability in the strict sense is not
impossibility, since much that is possible cannot be directly verified (p. 373). (5) So changeless time is
possible, and time is not just change. *Objections:* the freeze must end, and its end seems uncaused
(Shoemaker's own p. 367 problem, with Warmbrod 2004, not read); "McTaggartian" change, which he sets aside, may
count if the mere passage of time can be causally efficient (p. 379, a point credited to Barcan Marcus); and
relationists reformulate so that change is not needed (Morganti 2017, abstract only). Note footnote 7: in a local
freeze things still change in their relations to the unfrozen regions, so only a total freeze is a clean case.

**D. McTaggart** (full-text, selected). (1) Time involves change. (2) Change requires the A series. (3) A, B, C
terms: the B series (earlier, later) is permanent and cannot give change alone. (4) Past, present and future are
incompatible, yet each event has all three. (5) The "has been, will be" reply presupposes time, which the A
series was to explain, so the reply is circular. (6) So the A series, and with it time, is unreal. *Objection:*
deny (1) or (2), as B-theorists do. The notes in the tense-and-language lane record this.

**E. Ratnakīrti: momentariness from causal efficacy** (as reported in SEP). (1) A thing exists only if it can
produce an effect. (2) The ability is known only by seeing the effect. (3) The seed in the granary does not
produce the sprout, and the seed in the soil does. (4) So they differ, and no two moments of a thing are the
same. (5) So existents last for the shortest possible duration. *Objections:* the argument needs
atomic time, and the Sarvāstivāda own account of four phases within a moment infringes momentariness
(SEP Abhidharma, citing von Rospatt). Nyāya replies exist (Udayana) and were not read.

## 3. Live open problems

1. **Time without change in physical theory.** Can a changeless interval be stated in a relativistic or
   quantum theory? *Progress:* a worked model of a frozen sector with an end, or a proof that no such model
   is consistent, in a stated class.
2. **Relational versus substantival time in general relativity.** Does the metric count as a clock, or is a
   clock a body? *Progress:* a criterion for what extra structure a clock adds, accepted by both sides. The
   hole-argument literature (not read) is where it sits.
3. **Physical basis of duration.** Rugh and Zinkernagel ask what fixes a time scale where no scale-setting
   process exists (before the electroweak transition). *Progress:* a derivation of the scale from some
   physical process, or an agreed account of why none is needed.
4. **Absolute succession against relational duration** (Gryb–Thébault; the problem-of-time lane). *Progress:*
   a quantum formulation where the ordering survives.
5. **Whether any non-clock time has physical content** (Bergson, McTaggart). *Progress:* a precise form of
   the claim that duration or the A series does work a clock does not. No one has this yet in a form physicists accept.

## 4. What in this repository depends on this area

**The local-clock note** (research/stein-gr/local-clock/RESULT.md; main.tex Prop. `prop:clock`, Rem. `rem:clock`
in parts/becoming_energy.tex).

- *The conclusion "a shared present can be local only if some physical quantity changes monotonically" is
  loose, and should be restated.* What is proved is relative to a class of spacetimes. A local clock is a
  monotone function of germs of the metric, and its absence is equivalent to the main theorem on that class
  (Thm 4). Calling it a "physical quantity that changes" imports the relational tradition without argument. A
  curvature scalar is a geometric invariant, equal to a trace of the stress-energy only on shell. On a
  relational reading (Aristotle, Leibniz, Rugh–Zinkernagel) a clock is a physical process, so the note
  has not yet shown that its clocks are clocks in that sense.
- *Relation to Aristotle, as I read it, and a conjecture of ours.* Aristotle's time orders and measures
  change along bodies, and he adds, without argument, that the same time holds "everywhere at once"
  (IV.12). Relativity removes that addition. The note's conclusion then says what remains: a shared present of
  causally unrelated events needs a common monotone quantity, and where that quantity is constant (flat
  space, Scal = 0) there is none, while the local causal order is untouched. That pattern separates three
  things the tradition runs together: local order (needs only causal structure), shared present (needs a
  clock quantity), metrical duration (needs a clock process, per Rugh–Zinkernagel). It also locates Shoemaker's
  puzzle: the theorem is silent on whether a total freeze is possible. No source read draws this connection.
- *Who has linked relativity and the Aristotelian or relational tradition.* Rugh and Zinkernagel 2008 do so for
  cosmic time and cite Aristotle, Leibniz, Grünbaum, Smolin and Barbour (read through §2.2 only). Gryb and
  Thébault 2014 link positive lapse in general relativity to absolute succession. Rovelli's paper on Aristotle's
  *Physics* sets Aristotle's treatment of time aside, so it is not a source for this. I found none that ties Stein-type
  becoming theorems to Aristotle or to Shoemaker. Four or five searches were made, so this is no priority claim.

**main.tex §`sec:becoming-rival`, "The strongest rival: clock-equipped spacetimes"** (parts/becoming_phil.tex).
It says a clock definable from the metric "adds no structure" and that a clock not so definable is "added
structure". That is substantivalist bookkeeping. A relationist counts any clock as matter, and a Newtonian counts
a clock as time itself. The choice of category therefore carries the relational/substantival dispute, which the
paper leaves unmentioned. Add the sentence, and cite Newton, Leibniz–Clarke and Rugh–Zinkernagel.

**main.tex introduction and the "index" diagnostic** (Part IV abstract; §`sec:bounds`). "Defined, then trivial and
shared, or primitive" is close to McTaggart's dilemma for the A series, and the fragmentalist remark (line 610) is
uncited. The paper never cites McTaggart, Bergson or Shoemaker. This is a gap, not an error. The tense-and-language
lane has the reading.

**main.tex §`sec:process`** ("Constitution and process"). It compares process mereology with event realism. The
reductive end, momentariness, where persisting processes are constructed over momentary events, is a rival
reading that is not discussed. Optional.

**main.tex §`sec:direction`, fixing-exchange note, horizons notes.** No dependence on this lane found. They take the
time orientation as an input. Nothing read here shows an error in them.

## 5. Ten primary sources to read next

1. **Rugh and Zinkernagel 2008, full text and published version.** Settles whether "physical basis of time order"
   is a defended thesis, and how much of the local-clock note it already says.
2. **Gryb and Thébault 2014 (arXiv:1303.7139), full text.** Settles whether monotone lapse is a real
   structure or a gauge choice.
3. **Shoemaker 1969 in an authorised copy, with Warmbrod 2004.** Settles the cause-of-restart objection.
4. **Morganti 2017, full text** (the PhilArchive copy was blocked). Settles whether relationism survives
   changeless intervals.
5. **Coope 2001 and Corish on Aristotle.** Settle whether "no time without change" is Aristotle's thesis.
6. **Earman, *World Enough and Space-Time* (1989).** Settles the relational/substantival state of play after
   general relativity.
7. **Bergson, *Durée et simultanéité*, with Einstein's 1922 remarks and Canales (2015).** Settle what Bergson
   claimed; the one reading I made (Unnikrishnan) is contested.
8. **Smolin 1999 and Grünbaum 1977**, as cited by Rugh and Zinkernagel. Settle whether a clock-based
   relationism in general relativity is already stated.
9. **Von Rospatt 1995 and Udayana's reply.** Settle the content of the Nyāya critique of momentariness.
10. **Clarke's and Leibniz's original text**, to check Bennett's abridgement.
