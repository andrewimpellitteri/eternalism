## 1. The main positions

**McTaggart's A- and B-series (McTaggart, "The Unreality of Time," *Mind* 1908; full-text,
Wikisource transcription).** Positions in time carry permanent earlier/later order (the B series)
and non-permanent past/present/future position (the A series). McTaggart holds the A series
essential: "the distinction of past, present and future is as essential to time as the distinction
of earlier and later", and argues the A series is contradictory, so time is unreal. The C series
(undirected order) plus an A series generates time; a B series cannot exist without an A series.

**The tensed (A) theory with tensed facts.** A-theorists hold that temporal reality includes
A-determinations, and some that tensed sentences express tensed facts. Prior ("Thank Goodness
That's Over," *Philosophy* 34, 1959; full-text) argues tensed discourse is ineliminable: the
object of relief is a tensed fact, not a dated one. Quentin Smith (*Language and Time*, OUP 1993;
preface and ch. 1 opening, partial full-text) states his version: the tensed theory "implies ...
that 'now' ... conveys that the sun's shining has the property of presentness"; he defends it
against Mellor, Oaklander *and* the New Theory of Reference, by the untranslatability of tensed
by tenseless sentences plus coherence and scientific viability.

**The old tenseless theory.** Tensed sentences are translatable into tenseless, dated ones.
Named here by its target: Prior 1959 attacks N. L. Wilson's substance-language, in which "a
is blue at t" with a tenseless copula is the basic form. Smart is the usual named defender;
his texts were not read, so the position is stated from Prior's and Mellor's characterisations
of it (Mellor: "older B-theories that try to translate or dispense with A-statements").

**The new tenseless theory.** Mellor (*Real Time II*, Routledge 1998; front matter and
Introduction, full-text): "There is in reality no such thing as being past, present or future."
Tensed sentences are untranslatable, but their meaning is "a function from any B-time t to its
B-truthmaker at t" ('e is past' is true at t iff e is earlier than t); A-beliefs are needed only
as timely belief states of agents, and passage is "the psychological reality behind the
A-theorist's myth of the flow of time". Oaklander (Phil. Studies 58, 1990; abstract) defends the
token-reflexive version against Smith by rejecting Smith's methodological assumptions.

**Tense logic.** Prior's tense logic treats tense as primitive operators on propositions;
branching time represents the open future (Past, Present and Future 1967, ch. 7, unread here;
secondary: SEP "Temporal Logic", Goranko and Rumberg, rev. 2024, §5). Peircean and Ockhamist
variants differ on whether a claim's truth is settled on all future branches or on the actual
branch. Goldblatt's S4.2 result for Minkowski frames (cited in the repo) belongs to this line.

**Essential indexicality.** Perry ("The Problem of the Essential Indexical," Noûs 13, 1979;
full-text): belief attributions explain action through belief *states*, and locating beliefs
("I am making a mess", "the meeting starts now") resist capture by any object of belief —
descriptive, de re, or relativised (truth-at-a-person-and-time). Kaplan ("Demonstratives,"
1989; TOC and preface read) splits character from content: indexicality is in the rule from
context to content, so contents themselves can be timeless. This is the machinery the new
tenseless theorists use against tensed facts.

**Aspect and event structure (Vendler, Comrie, Dowty, Moens–Steedman).** Vendler ("Verbs and
Times," Phil. Review 66, 1957; pp. 143–146 full-text, rest unread) classifies verbs by their
time schemata: states (no progressive), activities (progressive; "for a time"), accomplishments
(terminal point: "if someone stops running a mile, he did not run a mile"; "in an hour"),
achievements (punctual). Dowty (*Ling. and Phil.* 9, 1986; §§1–2 full-text) shows aspectual
class governs narrative time movement: accomplishments advance, states and progressives
overlap and "resist the inceptive interpretation"; aspectual class is semantic and
knowledge-dependent, which he argues against Kamp–Hinrichs discourse rules. Moens and
Steedman (*Computational Linguistics* 14(3), 1988, pp. 15–28; full-text) refine the taxonomy
(culmination, point, culminated process, process, state) and define the **nucleus**: "a
structure comprising a culmination, an associated preparatory process, and a consequent
state". The **perfect** predicates of the reference time that the consequent state holds; the
**progressive** "demands that its argument be a process, which it predicates as ongoing";
modifiers coerce aspectual type. Tense is anaphoric on a Reichenbachian reference time.

**Processes (Steward, Stout, Galton).** Stout ("Processes," *Philosophy* 72, 1997; publisher
extract read): events have temporal parts, but processes "do not extend in time and do not
have temporal parts, but rather persist in time"; the event category is not basic. Steward
(*Mind* 122, 2013; abstract read) opposes the continuant view of processes while vindicating
the intuitions behind it — that processes are individuals with distinctive explanatory roles.
Galton (*The Logic of Aspect*, 1984; book page read, discovery) gives an axiomatic tense-logic
treatment of aspect.

## 2. Canonical arguments

**(a) McTaggart: time is unreal.** (1) Time involves change (else "a timeless universe").
(2) Without an A series, the only candidate changes — events beginning/ceasing to be events,
one event becoming another, moments changing — all fail, since B relations are permanent.
(3) So all change is change in A-determinations of events. (4) Past, present, future are
"incompatible determinations... no event can be more than one" (essential to their meaning).
(5) "Every event has them all" (each has been, is, or will be each). (6) The obvious reply —
"present, has been future, and will be past" — hides a second A series in "has been"/"will
be"; a "vicious circle", or a regress of A series. (7) Treating A-determinations as qualities
(futurity, presentness, pastness) fares no better. Conclusion: the A series is contradictory,
so time is unreal. *Standard objections.* The B-theorist denies (1)–(3): change is different
incompatible tenseless properties at different B-times (SEP "Time," Emery, Markosian,
Sullivan, rev. 2025, §5). Against the regress, Mellor's move is to accept McTaggart's
contradiction as an existence-proof that there are *no A-properties*, while keeping B-facts
and truthmakers (RT II Introduction, verified); his chapter-length reply (ch. 7) is unread.

**(b) Perry: the essential indexical.** (1) Belief attributions that explain action identify
belief states. (2) In Perry's shopper, professor and hiker cases, replacing the indexical
("I", "now", "this") by any other designation of the same thing destroys the explanation
("I came to believe John Perry is making a mess" explains stopping only given the further
indexical belief "I am John Perry"). (3) No candidate object of belief works: descriptive
propositions fail; de re propositions are "nonindexical" and cannot capture self-location;
relativised propositions (true at ⟨person, time⟩) fail because believing one true *for oneself
then* can be shared with the inert shopper. (4) So belief states are not reducible to
relations to any propositional object; indexicals are essential to belief-state
characterisation. *Standard objection:* Kaplan's character/content distinction — indexicals
are essential to character (the context-to-content rule), not to content; a singular
proposition is what is believed. Whether that deflates indexicality or relocates it is the
live dispute Perry's postscripts address (not read).

**(c) Prior: "Thank goodness that's over."** (1) The tenseless analysis says tensed tokens
are translatable: relief at the pain's ending = relief that "the date of the conclusion of
that thing is Friday, June 15, 1954", or that it "is contemporaneous with this utterance".
(2) Prior: the first is not what anyone is glad of, and of the second he asks "Why should
anyone thank goodness for that?" — mere contemporaneity is no object of relief. (3) More
generally, the tense cannot "cross the copula": "having been green in August" is a present
property ("attaches to it now"), not a tenseless way of being green. Conclusion: tensed facts
or at least tensed propositions are ineliminable. *Standard objection:* Mellor's
token-reflexive truthmakers — what makes 'e is past' true at t is e's being earlier than t;
A-beliefs are needed for timely action (truth of a belief = success of the actions it
combines with desires to cause), so tensedness lives in belief states, not facts (RT II
Introduction, verified). The exchange is not closed: Smith's rejoinder is that
untranslatability plus coherence is *adequate evidence* for tensed facts (ch. 1, verified).

**(d) The new tenseless theory's argument (Mellor).** (1) 'e is past' cannot *mean* 'e is
earlier than t': the latter, if true, is always true; the former only after e. (2) But
truthmakers may still be tenseless: e's being earlier than t makes 'e is past' true at t (the
'I live in Cambridge' analogy). (3) If A-properties existed they would be what makes
A-statements true; since that is excluded (by McTaggart's contradiction, as Mellor reads it),
A-properties do not exist (the 'Nobody' analogy). (4) A-belief is nonetheless indispensable
for agents who must act at times; this explains, without A-facts, tensed meaning
(meaning-as-function), action and the illusion of flow. *Standard objection:* Smith: the
argument assumes the truthmaker/translation framework; his book argues the untranslatability
evidence points the other way (tensed content), against both token-reflexive and date
versions (Oaklander's 1990 reply disputes the methodological premises; abstract read).

**(e) The progressive and the imperfective paradox.** (1) Vendler: progressives of
accomplishments carry a completion claim — "if someone stops running a mile, he did not run
a mile"; states take no progressive. (2) Yet the progressive cannot entail completion:
"the clock on the wall ticked loudly"-style states and "was writing a letter" overlap rather
than advance narrative time (Dowty 1986, verified), and progressives "resist the inceptive
interpretation". (3) Moens–Steedman name the resulting problem the "imperfective paradox"
(classic formal statement: Dowty 1979, unread): the progressive is not an existential
quantifier over completed events, so "is building a house" can hold with no house ever
built. (4) Their answer: the progressive views the *preparatory process* of a nucleus as
ongoing; coercion explains odd forms. *Standard objections:* modal/stage analyses (e.g.
Landman 1992, Portner 1998 — named in the literature, unread) predict different truth
conditions for "was crossing the street when hit"; Dowty's own data (continued-occurrence
readings) remain the empirical test for any semantics.

## 3. Live open problems

1. **Tensed facts vs token-reflexive belief states** (the Mellor–Oaklander–Smith dispute).
   Progress: a semantics that both predicts tensed action-explanation (Perry's data) and
   settles the ontological question, or a precise expressive-inadequacy theorem for
   token-reflexive contents (Smith's untranslatability made formal).
2. **McTaggart's regress.** Progress: consistent, expressively adequate formal models of
   changing A-facts (Fine's relativism; Lipman's standpoints; the repo's
   `local-fragmentalism` lane) versus a proof that any such model re-instantiates the
   regress at the metalanguage.
3. **The imperfective paradox.** Progress: a compositional semantics of the progressive
   predicting (i) non-entailment of completion, (ii) Vendler's continuation implication for
   accomplishment progressives, and (iii) Dowty's discourse data (progressives block
   narrative advance).
4. **Tense as anaphora across languages** (Partee, via Moens–Steedman §4.1). Is the
   Reichenbachian reference time universal, or do aspect-prominent languages track reference
   differently? Progress: cross-linguistic formal semantics (e.g. Guaraní-type systems).
5. **Tense logic of relativistic frames.** The repo's own open question
   (`parts/logic_remark.tex`: whether S4 is exactly the logic of all past-directed globally
   hyperbolic frames) sits in a live literature: Goldblatt's S4.2 for Minkowski; Lewis and
   van der Schaaf (2026, cited in the repo) extend causal modal logics. Progress:
   completeness and decidability results for natural classes of spacetime frames.
6. **Future contingents and the open future.** Peircean vs Ockhamist vs supervaluationist
   treatments of "will" (SEP Temporal Logic §5; Prior 1967 ch. 7, unread). Progress: a
   semantics matching both ordinary future-tense use and the bivalence policies the repo's
   Part IV separates.

## 4. What in this repository depends on this area

The project's four-way split — has become / is fixed / exists / is true (README;
`main.tex`, Setting table, abstract) — is *grammatically* well-motivated: the aspect
literature confirms these are distinct categories, not one notion. But specific uses need
revision, defence or repair, as follows.

**(a) "Has become for b" (`main.tex` abstract and Setting table row for B_M(b);
`becoming_phil.tex`).** "Has become" is the present perfect of an achievement verb. By the
standard analysis (Moens–Steedman, verified; Reichenbach S/E/R as they report it), the
perfect asserts of the reference time — here the standpoint b — that a *consequent state*
of a culmination holds; the reference time is anaphoric, so the expression is standpoint-
relative *by construction*. Two consequences. First, the gloss carries no content beyond
the relation x ≤ b: it cannot be evidence that becoming is a primitive relation, and it
imports an event-complex ontology (preparatory process, culmination, consequent state) that
bare manifold points lack. This is not an error, but the paper should mark the gloss as a
metaphor and state that the content is the relation, as its own limits section partly does
("Reading B_M as existence from b is a further bridge"). Second, "become" (unlike "occur")
semantically embeds a change into a state; using it for all of J⁻(b) overstates what a
causal-relation theorem delivers. Suggested wording: "what has happened by b" for the
relation, reserving "has become" for the bridge.

**(b) "Is fixed" (`main.tex` Parts II–III passim; README; `fixing-exchange/RESULT.md`).**
English "is fixed" is a stative resultative participle: it denotes a resultant state at
speech time of a fixing event (the Parsons-style resultative analysis — standard in event
semantics; Parsons 1990 unread). The project's F_L is a different, *modal* notion: agreement
across all admitted completions, which branching-time logic makes explicit with a
settled-truth operator (Belnap et al. 2001, as cited in SEP Temporal Logic §5). The word
"fixed" thus spans three senses — resultant state, settledness across completions, and the
fixing-exchange note's determination relation between regions — and the README slogan
("what is fixed") invites the stative reading. Nothing here is false; but one sentence in
Part II distinguishing these senses would prevent a predictable misreading. The
cone-algebras note already keeps "fixed" apart from correlation — the right instinct.

**(c) The missing progressive.** The project has a perfective relation ("has become") and
states ("is fixed"), but no progressive/underway relation, while its process section
(`sec:process`, citing Stout 1997 and Steward 2013) argues about ongoing processes (the
filling-device comparison). Steward's own case is grounded in the continuant-like behaviour
of processes *in progress* — exactly the category Moens–Steedman isolate as the progressive's
input. By the standards of this literature the section is naive in one respect: it treats
process explanation as a matter of causal organisation alone, without noting that processes
have distinctive aspectual grammar (ongoing, unfinished, "is going on") that no culmination
claim captures. Either add a standpoint-relative "underway at b" relation (events whose
preparatory process spans b) or say explicitly that no such category is claimed.

**(d) Truth policies (`sec:truth`).** "Fix a claim q at b about a reading at a later event"
treats future-talk as one class. English future constructions are not: predictive "will" vs
futurate progressive/going-to differ in aspect (Moens–Steedman tie futurates to preparatory
processes and intention). The policies L and S are fine as regimentation; the section should
say they apply to regimented outcome claims, not to English future constructions, which
carry aspect and modality the policies do not model.

**(e) "News" (`horizons-and-news/RESULT.md`).** The note's "news" is the literal truth of a
standard linguistic analysis: the perfect's "hot news" reading is one consequent state among
others (Moens–Steedman, verified, crediting McCawley 1971 and Comrie 1976). The horizon
result gives that reading non-metaphorical content (data not yet arrived). Worth one citation.

**(f) Tense logic (`parts/logic_remark.tex`).** The Diodorean reading, S4.2 on Minkowski
frames (Goldblatt, cited), and the open S4 question are properly placed in the Prior–
Goldblatt lineage; nothing to revise, but this is where part 3, item 5 would bite first.

**(g) One empirical claim is wrong as stated.** The introduction says: "In ordinary thought
these are nearly one question" (about has-become, is-fixed, exists, is-true). The aspect
literature shows ordinary *language* already distinguishes all four (perfect, resultative
state, existential, evaluative predicate); what runs them together is a metaphysical habit,
not the grammar. Restate the claim about thought, or drop it.

**(h) Adjacent project notes.** `local-fragmentalism/redteam/03_fragmentalist_relational.md`
already distinguishes "settled for b" from "has become for b" — precisely the
state/perfect distinction this literature draws. The paper should import that distinction
explicitly.
