# Agency, fatalism and the open future — reading note

11 November 2026. GLM reading lane `read-agency-fatalism-open-future`. Read depths are
labelled: full-text = the named sections of the accessed document were read directly;
secondary-full-text = same, for a secondary source; discovery = metadata or citation
chasing only. Nothing below quotes more than a short phrase; citations are paraphrases
with locators. This note is a submission for review, not a settled truth claim.

## 1. The main positions

**Aristotelian gap.** For future contingents the disjunction is necessary while neither
disjunct is determinately true now; where there is "real alternatives" (two-sided
potentiality), the affirmation is no more true than the denial, and deliberation and
action are causative for the future. Defender: Aristotle, *On Interpretation* ch. 9
(Bekker 18b23 ff. as located by Øhrstrøm & Hasle 2024; Edghill translation read
full-text on MIT Classics). The attribution is contested: Markosian (1995, full-text)
says it "at least appears" that Aristotle took this line.

**Łukasiewicz trivalent.** Future contingents take the third value "undetermined"
today. Defender: Łukasiewicz (1920s–30s), whose Warsaw example ("can at the present
time be neither true nor false") is quoted in Torre 2011 (full-text). Known here only
at secondary depth (SEP; Torre).

**Peirceanism.** The future operator *F* is the necessary future: true at t iff true in
every history through t, so every future contingent is false now. Defender: Prior
(*Past, Present and Future*, 1967, as expounded in Øhrstrøm & Hasle 2024, secondary
full-text). Prior's motive is agency: "it wasn't going to be the case until that agent
decided that it was" (Prior, *Some Free Thinking about Time*, quoted via Copeland 1996
in the SEP entry). The system's cost: no plain future distinct from the possible and
the necessary, and *F¬q* is not equivalent to *¬Fq*.

**Ockhamism.** Truth is evaluated at a moment *within* a history: *Fφ* is true at t on
history h iff φ true later on h. Future contingents therefore have plain (history-
relative) truth now, and the necessity of the past (P3) fails for "soft" facts about
the past that depend on the future. Defender: Prior 1967 (system named for Ockham's
*Tractatus de praedestinatione*); Belnap's "settled" versus "plain" truth is, as the
SEP entry notes, close to Priorian Ockhamism (Belnap et al. 2001). Standard cost: it
is unclear what makes a past-tensed soft fact true (the SEP entry states the objection;
Plantinga 1986 defends it).

**Supervaluationism.** True at t iff true on every history through t; false at t iff
false on every history through t; otherwise a gap, while future excluded middle
*F q ∨ F ¬q* is supertrue. Defender: Thomason 1970 (primary paywalled; clauses known
here at secondary-full-text via Øhrstrøm & Hasle 2024, which also records the
truth-functional breakdown: a disjunction true with neither disjunct true).

**True futurism / thin red line.** One branch at every moment (including counterfactual
moments) is privileged, restoring bivalent truth now without nomic necessitation.
Defenders: McKim and Davis 1976, Thomason and Gupta 1980, and recent defences
(Borghini and Torrengo 2013; Iacona 2013) — all known here only as named by Øhrstrøm &
Hasle and Torre (discovery). Critic: Belnap and Green 1994, who argue the required
TRL-function is either inconsistent with branching or arbitrary.

**Assessment relativism.** Sentence truth is relativized to a context of utterance and
a context of assessment ("double time reference"): Jake's utterance is neither true nor
false as assessed when made, true as assessed from the battle, false as assessed from
no battle. Defender: MacFarlane 2003 (author offprint, full-text, §§I–VI).

**OF-compatibilism (open future with determinate truth now).** Openness is a modal
question (not everything *must* happen), not a failure of bivalence. Prototype: Lewis's
counterfactual-dependence account — the open future is the many futures that would come
about under suppositions about the present, the fixed past the one past that would
remain (Lewis's *Counterfactuals* p. 38 as quoted in Torre 2011, full-text). Barnes and
Cameron defend a companion line: the open future thesis is compatible with bivalence,
with determinism, and with future ontology (author PDF, full-text for §§0, 2, 3a).

**Open past.** If openness is defended non-question-beggingly, via nomic indeterminism
plus a tensed correspondence truth theory, the past is open too: time-symmetric laws are
indeterministic toward the past, and by TCT ("p is true at t just in case p corresponds
to the world at t") nothing now makes past-tensed contingents true or false. Defender:
Markosian 1995 (author PDF, full-text).

**Agency-side accounts of the asymmetry.** Torre (2011, full-text) proposes that the
openness asymmetry is tied to an asymmetry in power over: we have power over the future
and not the past. Ismael (2012, full-text) grounds it in the deliberative standpoint of
the embedded agent: from one's own point of view one's avowals are "self-fulfilling
performances", so the future is potential and the past hard fact; both the A-theorist's
reification and the B-theorist's dismissal are mistakes. Kutach (2011 handbook chapter,
author-distributed excerpt) treats the fixity of the past as pragmatic: past and future
are metaphysically on par, and folk wisdom mistakes an asymmetry of *useful* influence
for an asymmetry of influence.

**Incompatibilism.** Determinism (state-plus-laws entailment) is incompatible with the
ability to act otherwise. Defender: van Inwagen (§2 below). The asymmetry question is
independent of free will in both directions (Barnes–Cameron; Torre), but the shared
engine — "no one has power over the past or the laws" — is the same transfer pattern the
fatalist uses, which is why the sea-battle and free-will debates keep importing each
other's premises.

**The special question — what is the asymmetry, and of what kind?** Aristotelianism:
semantic gap grounded in ontic potentiality. Łukasiewicz: purely semantic. Peirceanism:
the asymmetry is nomic-agential, cashed semantically (contingent futures false).
Ockhamism and supervaluationism: semantic (plain truth/settledness distinctions over a
fixed modal space); on neither is the future ontologically thinner. True futurism: ontic
(a privileged branch). MacFarlane: semantic-pragmatic (assessment contexts). Barnes–
Cameron: ontic (metaphysical indeterminacy), explicitly *not* nomic (compatible with
determinism) and *not* semantic (bivalence retained). Markosian: nomic plus semantic
(TCT). Torre, Ismael, Kutach: practical/agency-based; Torre argues it is not merely
epistemic (the amnesia case), Ismael argues it is neither ontic inventory nor mere
ignorance, Kutach that it is definitional-pragmatic. Lewis: modal-causal (counterfactual
dependence). No account makes the asymmetry a theorem of the laws alone; that is also
what this repository's own exchange results say about exact fixing.

## 2. Canonical arguments, with objections

**(a) Logical fatalism (sea battle).** Primary: Aristotle, *De Interpretatione* 9;
formal reconstruction (secondary, Øhrstrøm & Hasle 2024 §2, echoing Lavenham's
*De eventu futurorum*): (P1) *F(y)p ⊃ P(x)F(x)F(y)p* — if it will be, it was true that
it would be; (P2) □(P(x)F(x)p ⊃ p); (P3) *P(x)p ⊃ □P(x)p* — necessity of the past;
(P4) K-pattern modal inference; (P5) *F(x)p ∨ F(x)¬p* — future excluded middle. Then:
(1)–(2) give *F(y)q ⊃ □F(y)q*; (7) P5; (8) *□F(y)q ∨ □F(y)¬q* — no future contingents.
Standard exits: reject P5 (Łukasiewicz; Peirceanism), reject P3 for soft facts
(Ockhamism), reject bivalence without denying the disjunction (supervaluationism),
reject the absoluteness of utterance-truth (MacFarlane 2003). A further standard
objection targets the inference itself: OF-compatibilists hold the argument trades on
confusing what will be with what must be (Torre 2011).

**(b) Markosian's open-past argument (1995).** Premises: (1) the laws are
indeterministic with regard to the future (some world-state has no unique lawful
successor); (2) the laws are (mostly) time-symmetric, hence indeterministic with regard
to the past as well; (3) TCT, the tensed correspondence theory: p true at t iff p
corresponds to the world at t. Conclusion: some past-tensed propositions about what
obtained are, now, neither true nor false — the past is sometimes open; so the only
non-question-begging defence of the open future forces an open past. (Markosian's own
qualifications: possibility, not actuality; "open" is technical and does not assert
power to change the past.) Standard objection: TCT's correspondence-now requirement is
exactly what bivalence-preserving views deny — Barnes and Cameron hold truths need no
present world-corresponding ground (metaphysical indeterminacy across precisifications),
and Briggs–Forbes (read in this repository's growing-block lane, full-text 2026-10-27)
supply laws as truthmakers, avoiding the gap.

**(c) van Inwagen's main argument (1975).** Primary: "The Incompatibility of Free Will
and Determinism", *Philosophical Studies* 27, 185–199 (author copy read full-text).
Judge case: determinism = (a) each instant has a state-proposition, (b) any two state-
propositions plus the laws entail each other. With P0 the state before J's birth, P the
state at T, L the law-conjunction: (1) if determinism, P0∧L entails P; (2) if J had
raised his hand at T, P would be false; (3) if J could have raised his hand, J could
have rendered P false; (4) if J could render P false and P0∧L entails P, J could render
P0∧L false; (5) if J could render P0∧L false, J could render L false (transfer past
irrelevance); (6) J could not render L false (no law is renderable false); (7) if
determinism, J could not have raised his hand. Standard objections: the conditional
analysis of "could" (van Inwagen's Part IV dilemma reply: either the analysed premises
are true, giving a sound argument, or they are counterexamples to the analysis); Lewis's
1981 reply that no one can break the laws yet could have done otherwise only by a
"local miracle" (known here via the SEP Compatibilism entry, secondary-full-text, which
also records Slote's context-sensitivity objection to the transfer inference).

**(d) Barnes and Cameron against "settled by the present" (2011).** Their target
argument (§1a): (1) either it is true that there will be a sea battle or true that there
will not; (2) truth is timeless in this sense — what is true simpliciter is true now;
(3) if true now, then how tomorrow is is settled by how the present is; (4)/(5) so the
present settles everything. Their reply: determinism makes only the *conditional* "if
the state at t and laws L, then future F" metaphysically necessary; the consequent is
settled only if the antecedent is, and there can be unsettledness in the antecedent
(state or, on regularity theories, in which laws obtain). So determinism does not entail
a closed future, and truth now does not require future ontology. Standard objection:
from the power side — Tooley's "what can be prevented at a given time is not yet a fact
at that time" (1997, 44–45, quoted in Torre 2011) — settlement should follow
preventability, not precisifications; the dispute is live.

**(e) Belnap's branching space-time (1992 postprint).** Postulates: ≤ a partial order
on possible point events (2-1), no backward branching; histories are maximal directed
subsets (3-2); a point event is indeterministic iff the finest partition of histories
through it respecting "no choice between obviously undivided histories" has more than
one member (5-2, 5-6); the prior choice principle: if e lies in h1−h2, some choice point
for h1, h2 lies in e's past (7-1). Consequences: the "wings" (causal contemporaries of a
choice point) must lie in the intersection h1∩h2 (8-3) — splitting is local, happens at
point events not slices, and "affects only the causal future". Fact 13-15: yes — given
the entire proper past of a *point*, its occurrence is settled; no — given the entire
past of a *chain without a last member*, what happens next is not. Standard objections/
limits: no probabilities are built into BST-92 (Weiner's suggestions are noted in its
references); and the history definition does not transfer wholesale to GR (Placek 2014;
Belnap–Müller–Placek 2021 — both read at clause level by this repository's
relativistic-branching lane).

## 3. Live open problems and what would count as progress

1. **OF-compatibilism versus OF-incompatibilism.** Is determinate truth now compatible
   with genuine openness? Progress: a agreed, formal notion of "settled" (e.g. a
   settlement operator with axioms both sides accept) plus one worked case each side
   concedes is decisive. Torre 2011 frames exactly this; the repository's policy L/S
   split is an instance.
2. **Grounding present future-truth.** Four live options: correspondence now (Markosian
   TCT); laws as truthmakers (Briggs–Forbes); metaphysical indeterminacy without future
   grounds (Barnes–Cameron); truth-value gaps (Thomason). Progress: a test able to
   separate them — e.g. this repository's coordinator question (is there a
   non-question-begging reason present truth must supervene on existing event
   structure?) answered against all four named options rather than against one.
3. **The source of the past/future asymmetry.** Candidates: semantic, nomic, epistemic,
   ontic, practical (power/deliberation), modal-counterfactual. Progress: a mapping
   table stating, for each candidate source, exactly which asymmetry it delivers
   (truth-value status; power; records; counterfactual dependence; exact fixing) and
   which it leaves untouched. This is where this repository could contribute: its
   exchange and balance results show exact fixing carries no law-borne arrow, so any
   truth- or agency-asymmetry must come from another column of the table.
4. **Branching for general relativity.** BST histories do not transfer wholesale;
   generalized-history definitions exist (Placek 2014; BMP 2021). Progress: one
   concrete GR model shown to satisfy a generalized history definition with its
   choice-point structure exhibited — the repository's closed-dust model is a candidate
   test bed.
5. **Assessment-sensitive truth.** The Evans challenge (correctness varying with
   assessment time seems incoherent) is answered in MacFarlane 2003 §VI but the
   post-2003 debate continues. Progress: an accepted account of assertion norms under
   a-contextuality (MacFarlane's 2014 book is the arena).
6. **Aristotle exegesis** (which solution ch. 9 endorses) — live in the literature but
   low priority for this repository, which only cites the argument's shape.

## 4. What in this repository depends on this area

**`research/what-the-past-can-fix/main.tex`**

- **§Truth (sec:truth, lines 506–524).** Policies L and S are, in this literature,
   respectively a designated-realization (thin-red-line-style) bivalent clause and
   Thomason-style supervaluationism over admitted completions. The section says "the
   logical point belongs to an established debate" but cites none of it: Prior,
   Thomason, MacFarlane and Belnap-branching-time are absent from the paper's
   bibliography (Belnap 1992 is cited only in Part II for BST). Two revisions follow:
   (i) name the debate and the two policies' ancestors (L: Ockhamist/TRL;
   S: Thomason 1970), and state the costs the literature records for S — falsity must
   be construed as contrary rather than contradictory for gaps to be stable (Markosian
   1995, n. 7), and supertruth makes a disjunction true with neither disjunct true
   (Øhrstrøm & Hasle 2024 §5.4); (ii) the menu is incomplete: MacFarlane's
   assessment-relative policy is a third option in which the verdict at b depends on a
   later standpoint, which would interact with the paper's claim that the physics fixes
   the comparison while the policy is chosen at b. Not wrong — the paper's explicit
   framing as "restricted counterinterpretation, not a model of any named theory" is
   exactly the right caution — but incomplete by the field's standards.
- **§The direction of time (sec:direction, lines 526–578).** The claim that the mirror
   proposition leaves "past fixed, future open" without a source is *confirmed* by this
   literature: Markosian 1995 and Torre 2011 both argue indeterminism alone yields an
   open past as well (Torre cites Lewis 1986b for the point). But the paper says "We
   know of three candidates" — thermodynamic (Albert), perspectival (Price), causal
   (Tooley). By the field's standard taxonomy this list misses at least three:
   the counterfactual-dependence account (Lewis), the power-over account (Torre 2011),
   and the deliberative/pragmatic accounts (Ismael 2012; Kutach 2011). The candidate
   list should be widened or the scope of "candidates" restricted; as it stands the
   sentence is naive about the size of the option space.
- **§Which inferences fail (sec:revise), item "'Past fixed, future open' as evidence
   for growth."** The reasoning — the record asymmetry has an explanation using no
   inventory distinction, so it is not evidence for growth until that explanation fails
   — is consistent with, and strengthened by, Ismael 2012 and Kutach 2011, who give the
   strongest versions of exactly that explanation. The item currently cites no source
   for the candidate explanation; Ismael and Kutach are the citations to add.
- **Part II, fixing_guaranteed (Belnap remark, parts/fixing_guaranteed.tex lines
   39–41).** "Neither notion is Belnap's" is verified against the BST-92 postprint:
   histories are maximal *directed* sets (Def 3-2), not domains of dependence, and
   settledness for contemporaries (Fact 8-3) is not a guarantee theorem. Two additions
   the literature suggests: (i) BST-92 Fact 13-15 — a point's whole proper past settles
   it, a last-member-less chain's whole past does not settle what comes next — is the
   order-theoretic analogue of the paper's pointed-base versus slice-base distinction
   and deserves a citation beside the paper's same-past theorems; (ii) the paper's
   diagnostic that an index is "defined, and then trivial…, or primitive, and then a
   thesis the event realist rejects" parallels Belnap and Green's thin-red-line dilemma
   (a definable TRL leaves no branching; a primitive one begs the question); citing it
   would show the diagnostic is not ad hoc.

## 5. Ten primary sources to read next, ranked

1. **Thomason, "Indeterminist time and truth-value gaps", *Theoria* 36 (1970)
   264–281.** Paywalled this session. Would settle: the original supervaluationist
   clauses, his argument that indeterminism leaves no true future, and the restricted-
   substitution problem (p. 280) that any branching semantics here must answer.
2. **Prior, *Past, Present and Future* (1967), chs. on the Ockhamist and Peircean
   systems.** Would settle: the primary form of both systems and Prior's own
   agency-based preference, so policy L/S genealogy in sec:truth rests on primaries.
3. **Belnap, Perloff and Xu, *Facing the Future* (2001).** Would settle: stit agency,
   settled versus plain truth, double time references, and the Belnap–Green TRL
   constraints in their final form — the semantics-to-agency bridge the special
   question needs.
4. **Belnap and Green, "Indeterminism and the Thin Red Line", *Philosophical
   Perspectives* 8 (1994) 365–388.** Would settle: the precise argument that a
   definable TRL collapses branching; direct precedent for the paper's
   defined-or-primitive diagnostic.
5. **van Inwagen, *An Essay on Free Will* (1983), ch. III.** Would settle: the
   canonical N/P/laws operator formulation and the transfer principles behind premise
   (5)-(6) of the 1975 argument, including rule β.
6. **Lewis, "Are We Free to Break the Laws?", *Theoria* 47 (1981) 113–121.** Would
   settle: the standard compatibilist reply (local miracles) and whether the past-fixed
   premise survives it — needed for balance in sec:direction's candidate list.
7. **Tooley, *Time, Tense, and Causation* (1997), chs. on actuality as of a time.**
   Would settle: the strongest ontic/nomic version of the openness asymmetry that
   sec:direction's "causal" candidate stands for (currently cited in main.tex without
   a close reading anywhere in the repository).
8. **Kutach, *The Asymmetry of Influence* (2013).** Would settle: the "ensing"/
   decrement machinery behind the 2011 chapter, which grounds the influence asymmetry
   in statistical structure — the closest technical analogue to this repository's
   fixing calculus.
9. **MacFarlane, *Assessment Sensitivity* (2014).** Would settle: the final relativist
   postsemantics beyond the 2003 paper (which its author himself superseded) and the
   replies to the Evans challenge.
10. **Ismael, *How Physics Makes Us Free* (2016).** Would settle: her book-length
    compatibilism and "open system" picture of deliberation, the strongest
    physics-informed agency-side answer to the special question.
