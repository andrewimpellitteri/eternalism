\title{The universe as a thing:\\ a trilemma for Brading's P1\textsuperscript{**}}
\author{Working manuscript}
\date{21 October 2026}
\begin{document}
\maketitle

\begin{abstract}
Brading's local present is a spatiotemporal region of whatever size is needed to
sustain a dynamical system. Her P1\textsuperscript{**} requires every existing
thing's present to be less than the whole history, and she says that ``in order
to avoid the block universe, all we need is (a).'' This note grants her
identification of what exists with the genuine unities and her claim that the
law-constitutive approach applies to the material universe. It argues that the
universe case is underexplored. If the universe is a genuine unity and is in
P1\textsuperscript{**}'s domain, its sustaining region is either the whole
history, a proper region (spatially global or local), or outside the domain. On
the first, (a) is false of the universe; on the second, Brading's rejection of a
spatially global present needs qualification, or the local region must sustain
the universe's unity without the relations among all its members; on the third,
P1\textsuperscript{**}'s quantifier is restricted and the global claim is lost. The result is a trilemma, not a refutation:
P1\textsuperscript{**} cannot be both universal and satisfied by the universe
without an account of the universe's sustaining region that Brading does not
supply. The 2013 defeat condition and the 2015 survival condition treat
subsystem existence as the decisive empirical question, but the universe case is
independent of it. A four-event model also shows that, even for proper
subsystems, (a) and (b) do not imply a proper union. The verdict is limited:
Brading's sufficiency sentence fails, and the missing premise is an account of
how the universe's own present is fixed.
\end{abstract}

\section{The texts and the question}

Katherine Brading's \emph{Presentism as an Empirical Hypothesis} and
\emph{Physically Locating the Present} offer one of the most careful attempts to
keep a presentist ontology alive within special relativity
\citep{Brading2013,Brading2015}. She rejects the assumption that spacetime is the
ontological principle of unity of what exists, and replaces it with a
law-constitutive principle: the dynamical laws constitute genuine unities, and
the present of a system is the spatiotemporal region needed to sustain its
dynamics. The 2013 paper introduces the idea in a compact form:

\begin{quote}
The present is a spatiotemporal region of whatever size is necessary to sustain
the dynamical system in question \citep[p.~1109]{Brading2013}.
\end{quote}

The size is left open. It ``might turn out to be much, much smaller than the
abstract spatiotemporal structure within which the evolution of that unity is
completely described, or it might be the size of the block universe''
\citep[p.~1109]{Brading2013}. Brading then assumes genuine subsystems and draws
the local conclusion:

\begin{quote}
So let us begin from the position that there are genuine subsystems. This means
that the size of the spatiotemporal region required to sustain the system is
less than the entire block universe, and so the present is (at least in the
first instance) local, not global \citep[p.~1110]{Brading2013}.
\end{quote}

The 2015 paper states the position as P1\textsuperscript{**}:

\begin{quote}
(P1\textsuperscript{**}) For each and every thing that exists, that thing
exists only presently, where for each thing the spatiotemporal extent of the
present for that thing is dependent on the dynamics and is (a) less than the
entire spatiotemporal evolution of the universe as represented in Minkowski
spacetime, and (b) less than the entire spatiotemporal evolution of that thing
as represented in Minkowski spacetime \citep[p.~17]{Brading2015}.
\end{quote}

She adds: ``In order to avoid the block universe, all we need is (a)''
\citep[p.~17]{Brading2015}. Her own statement of the effect is weaker: a
smaller-than-universe present ``calls the ontology of a block universe into
question'' \citep[p.~17]{Brading2015}. She also leaves the empirical question
open: whether there are genuine subsystems ``smaller in spatiotemporal extent
than the entirety of spacetime'' is a matter of the details of the dynamics, and
``only if there are such can the version of presentism offered here survive''
\citep[p.~18]{Brading2015}.

This note asks what (a) establishes for the universe itself. The answer is a
trilemma, not a refutation. Granting Brading's identification of what exists
with the genuine unities, the universe is a candidate genuine unity. If it is in
P1\textsuperscript{**}'s domain, its present must be less than the whole history;
but the region needed to sustain it may be the whole history, a proper region
(spatially global or local), or outside the domain. Each option has a cost. If
the universe is excluded, the quantifier is restricted and the global claim is
lost. The sufficiency sentence ``all we need is (a)'' does not settle which
option holds.

The note does not claim that the coverage problem is wholly new, and it does not
compete with Savitt's 2021 objection. It claims that the universe case is a
distinct problem, that it is generated by Brading's own texts, and that her
survival condition does not address it. The formal model in \S\ref{sec:coverage}
is a second, independent problem for the subsystem family.

\section{What exists are the genuine unities}\label{sec:unity}

Brading's law-constitutive account is not only an account of the unity of
ordinary objects. She says that the principle applies across the board:

\begin{quote}
We are being offered a principle of ontological unity that applies across the
board to simple things, composite systems, and component parts of those
systems, and which applies all the way up to the material universe as a whole.
Insofar as what exists is what is one, the law-constitutive approach to unity is
a metaphysical thesis about what exists \citep[p.~16]{Brading2015}.
\end{quote}

The ``insofar as'' is a genuine conditional. Brading does not argue for the
law-constitutive approach here; she refers the reader to earlier work
\citep[p.~16]{Brading2015}. The unconditional formulation appears on the next
page: ``we begin from the premise that what exists (what is real) are the
genuine unities,'' and ``what is `present' depends on what is real (i.e. what
exists, the genuine unities), and what is real depends on the dynamical laws''
\citep[p.~17]{Brading2015}. The identification is a working premise of her
argument, not a theorem. This note therefore grants it. The question is what
follows for the universe if the premise is granted, not whether the premise is
true.

Granting the premise, two claims are needed for the universe to be a thing that
exists. The first is that the universe is a genuine unity. Brading's scope
claim---the principle applies ``all the way up to the material universe as a
whole''---gives the principle's intended scope, not the verdict that the
universe satisfies it. A principle of unity can apply to a collection and
deliver the verdict that the collection is not one. The textual support for the
verdict is elsewhere:

\begin{quote}
The dynamics grounds the unity of what there is, both of the parts and of the
whole (consisting of interacting parts) \citep[p.~1110]{Brading2013}.
\end{quote}

and the 2013 footnote says the law-constitutive approach covers ``the unity of
simple things, composite things, and the totality of existing things,''
applying to all three \citep[p.~1105, n.~3]{Brading2013}. The same footnote
adds that ``different principles of unity might be used for the different
cases,'' so the scope claim is not a uniform criterion. It is a licence to treat
the whole as a unity under some principle.

The second claim is that the universe satisfies the relevant principle. Brading
associates unity with the conservation of total quantity of motion when a system
is isolated from external interactions \citep[p.~1105]{Brading2013}, and with a
composite whole that conserves its total quantity and direction of motion
``when that system as a whole is free from external interactions''
\citep[p.~16]{Brading2015}. The universe is the limiting case of an isolated
system: there is no external interaction by definition. This is an argument, not
a quotation, and a reader may resist the application of the isolation condition
to the totality. The note treats the universe's unity as an interpretive premise
that Brading's texts support but do not compel. If the premise is rejected, the
universe falls outside the domain, which is the third horn of the trilemma.

\section{The universe's present: a trilemma}\label{sec:trilemma}

Grant the two premises: the universe is a genuine unity and a thing that exists.
P1\textsuperscript{**} is universal: ``for each and every thing that exists''
\citep[p.~17]{Brading2015}. What is the spatiotemporal extent of the universe's
present? Brading's proposal is that the present is the region ``of whatever size
is needed to sustain the dynamical system in question''
\citep[p.~17]{Brading2015}. The region needed to sustain the universe is not
fixed by the text. Three options are available, and each has a cost. Brading
uses ``local'' for any present that is a proper part of the block
\citep[p.~17]{Brading2015}. For the trichotomy below I use ``local'' more
narrowly, for a proper region that is not spatially global; the narrower sense
is the one at issue.

\paragraph{(1) The present is the whole history $\W$.} If the region needed to
sustain the universe is the entire spatiotemporal evolution, then the universe's
present is $\W$. Clause (a) requires every existing thing's present to be less
than $\W$. The universe is an existing thing. So (a) is false of the universe,
and P1\textsuperscript{**} is false on its unrestricted reading. This is the
strongest horn for the critic. Brading herself says that if a system's needed
region ``is the entire spatiotemporal history of the universe,'' then ``we are
back to the block universe'' \citep[p.~17]{Brading2015}. The universe is a
candidate system for which that description is apt.

\paragraph{(2) The present is a proper region.} A proper region avoids the
contradiction with (a). It divides into two sub-cases.

\emph{(2a) Spatially global.} A closed system's evolution is determined by its
state on a Cauchy slice, and the slice is a proper region of the history. If
``needed to sustain'' means ``the data needed to determine the evolution,'' the
universe's present could be a slice or a thick global slab, not $\W$. Its cost
is Brading's own rejection of a spatially global present. She writes that ``the
idea of a spatially global present common to all systems is not preserved in
this version of presentism, but the rejection of a spatially global present is
one of the central lessons of special relativity'' \citep[p.~17]{Brading2015}.
The qualifier ``common to all systems'' leaves room for a global present for the
universe alone, while subsystem presents are local and non-transitive. But the
second clause rejects a spatially global present without that qualifier. At a
minimum, Brading owes an account of why the universe's global slice is not the
global present she rejects. The text does not give one.

\emph{(2b) Local.} A local patch might sustain the universe's unity if the laws
determine the rest from the patch. Its cost is that the patch must sustain the
unity of a whole whose members are spread through the history. On Brading's
account of aggregation, ``the members must stand in relations to one another,
and those relations must be necessary and sufficient for the collection to form
a unity'' \citep[p.~16]{Brading2015}. If the relations are spatiotemporal and
span the history, a local patch omits them. If the relations are dynamical and
encoded in the patch, the patch can sustain the unity, but then the account owes
an explanation of how a local region encodes the relations among all members.
Brading does not give one. The sub-case is not excluded; it is underexplored.

\paragraph{(3) The universe is not in the domain.} If the universe is not a
genuine unity, or if ``thing'' is a term of art for proper unities below the
whole, then P1\textsuperscript{**} does not apply to it. This avoids the
trilemma by restricting the quantifier. Its cost is the global claim: P1\textsuperscript{**}
says ``for each and every thing that exists,'' and the universe is left out.
The view then says that every proper subsystem exists only presently, not that
the whole of reality does. That is a different thesis.

The three horns are exhaustive for the universe case: either it is in the domain
and its present is $\W$; or it is in the domain and its present is a proper
region, either spatially global or local; or it is not in the domain. No horn
yields P1\textsuperscript{**} as stated without a further account. Horn (1)
makes (a) false of the universe. Horn (2a) requires a qualification of Brading's
rejection of the global present. Horn (2b) requires an account of how a local
region sustains the whole. Horn (3) gives up the global claim.

The conclusion is therefore conditional, not categorical. P1\textsuperscript{**}
cannot be both universal and satisfied by the universe without an account of the
universe's sustaining region that Brading does not supply. The sufficiency
sentence ``all we need is (a)'' does not tell us which horn holds. The note does
not claim that no such account can be given; it claims that Brading does not
give one, and that the two obvious proper-region candidates each have a cost.

\section{The defeat condition and the survival condition}\label{sec:defeat}

Brading's 2013 defeat condition describes a different scenario:

\begin{quote}
It might turn out that, for whatever system we try to consider, the size of the
spatiotemporal region necessary to sustain it is the entire history of the
universe, encompassing all that ever has been and ever will be. In other words,
the only dynamical system that there is, is this entirety, and there are no
genuine subsystems of the universe. If that turns out to be the case, then this
version of presentism is defeated and the block universe triumphs
\citep[p.~1109]{Brading2013}.
\end{quote}

``For whatever system we try to consider'' is a universal quantifier. If every
system's region is $\W$, then no system has a proper region, so there are no
proper subsystems. The ``in other words'' is valid in that direction. It is not
an equivalence: the absence of proper subsystems does not entail that the only
system's region is $\W$; the only system might have a proper region.

The scenario in \S\ref{sec:trilemma} is different. The universe's region may be
$\W$ while subsystems have proper regions. The subsystems are parts of the
universe, not alternatives to it. Brading's defeat condition addresses the
universal scenario; it does not address the universe-own-region scenario. That
is not a logical defect in a sufficient condition. It is a gap in the framing.

The 2015 text makes the framing explicit:

\begin{quote}
Even were this approach to succeed, the crucial question would remain of whether
there are genuine subsystems of the world, as picked out by the dynamical laws,
that are smaller in spatiotemporal extent than the entirety of spacetime. Only
if there are such can the version of presentism offered here survive
\citep[p.~18]{Brading2015}.
\end{quote}

And 2013: ``A great deal rests on whether there are genuine subsystems of the
universe, and that is something that we can find out only through empirical
inquiry'' \citep[p.~1110]{Brading2013}. Brading treats the existence of
subsystems as the decisive empirical question. The universe case is independent
of it. Even if there are genuine subsystems, the universe remains a candidate
genuine unity with a present to fix. The survival condition is necessary for the
local presents of subsystems; it is not the question on which the truth of
P1\textsuperscript{**} turns.

Three scenarios should be kept apart:

\begin{enumerate}
\item \emph{Universal:} every system's region is $\W$; there are no proper
subsystems; Brading's stated sufficient condition for defeat is met.
\item \emph{Universe-own-region:} the universe's region is $\W$ while subsystems
have proper regions; the defeat condition is not met, but (a) is false of the
universe if it is in the domain.
\item \emph{Subsystem-only:} the universe is excluded or not a unity; subsystems
have proper regions; P1\textsuperscript{**} is restricted and the global claim
is given up.
\end{enumerate}

Brading's empirical question decides between (1) and (3). It does not decide
(2), which is the case that generates the trilemma.

\section{The retreat to subsystems and the coverage model}\label{sec:coverage}

The natural reply to the trilemma is horn (3): restrict
P1\textsuperscript{**} to proper subsystems. The retreat has one cost: it gives
up the global claim. P1\textsuperscript{**} says ``for each and every thing that
exists.'' If the universe is excluded, the quantifier is restricted, and the
view no longer says that every existing thing exists only presently. It says
that every proper subsystem does. That is a different thesis, not a reading of
the original.

Independently, a defender who keeps the global claim and tries to secure it
through (a) and (b) for subsystems faces a second problem: even for proper
subsystems, (a) and (b) do not imply a proper union.
Clause (a) says, for every system $i$,
\[
\U_i\subsetneq\W .
\]
It does not imply
\[
\bigcup_i \U_i\subsetneq\W .
\]

\begin{proposition}[Non-implication]\label{prop:one}
Local boundedness of every $\U_i$ is consistent with
$\bigcup_i\U_i=\W$.
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}
Take $\W=\{a,b,c\}$ and the proper subsets $\{a,b\}$ and $\{b,c\}$. Their union
is $\W$.
\end{proof}

The natural reply is that clause (b) closes the gap. Clause (b) says that each
thing's present is less than the entire spatiotemporal evolution of that thing.
That is a bound on the present relative to the thing's own history. It is not a
bound on the present relative to every other thing's present, and it is not a
claim that some event lies outside every present.

\begin{proposition}[(a) and (b) do not imply a proper union]\label{prop:two}
There is a model in which (a) and (b) hold for every system and
$\bigcup_i\U_i=\W$.
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}
Let $\W=\{a,b,c,d\}$. Let system 1 have history $\Hh_1=\{a,b,c\}$ and present
$\U_1=\{a,b\}$. Let system 2 have history $\Hh_2=\{b,c,d\}$ and present
$\U_2=\{c,d\}$. Then $\U_1\subsetneq\W$ and $\U_2\subsetneq\W$, so (a) holds.
Also $\U_1\subsetneq\Hh_1$ and $\U_2\subsetneq\Hh_2$, so (b) holds. But
$\U_1\cup\U_2=\{a,b,c,d\}=\W$.
\end{proof}

Three assumptions must be stated. First, the model is set-theoretic, not
dynamical: it does not show that $\U_i$ is the region \emph{necessary to
sustain} system $i$. It models the extensional shadow of (a) and (b). Second,
``less than'' is an extent comparison, and $\subsetneq$ is a regimenting choice;
it assumes containment, which is a charitable strengthening. Third, the model
uses a common event domain $\W$. Brading's 2013 note 7 denies determinate
ontological spatial or temporal relations between systems: ``All the ontology is
carried by the dynamics, and we frame the dynamics spatiotemporally, but
ontologically, the dynamics requires no such spatiotemporal underpinning''
\citep[p.~1109, n.~7]{Brading2013}. The model therefore treats $\W$ as the
common epistemic representation in which the comparison is stated, not as a
claim that the systems stand in determinate ontological relations. If no common
representation is allowed, the model is a result about the representation, not
about the ontology.

With those assumptions, the model shows that per-item properness is
coverage-neutral: (b) puts events outside their \emph{own} system's present, not
outside \emph{every} present. A defender who wants non-coverage needs an event
$e\in\W$ such that for all $i$, $e\notin\U_i$. Neither (a) nor (b) delivers one.
Non-coverage requires a joint constraint on the family, or a selection principle
that removes some presents. The model is a burden-shift, not a refutation of
Brading's dynamics.

The relation between the two problems is this. The trilemma concerns the
universe; the coverage model concerns the subsystem family. A defender who
restricts the quantifier to avoid the trilemma gives up the global claim. A
defender who keeps the global claim must answer the universe case, and the
coverage model shows that the subsystem clauses do not by themselves secure the
global restriction. The two problems are distinct, and neither is a substitute
for the other.

\section{Scale-general, not scale-free}\label{sec:scale}

The diagnosis is not that Brading lacks an existence criterion. She has one:
what exists are the genuine unities \citep[p.~17]{Brading2015}. Nor is the
criterion literally scale-free. Her 2013 note says ``different principles of
unity might be used for the different cases,'' while the law-constitutive
approach ``applies to all three'' \citep[p.~1105, n.~3]{Brading2013}. The
approach is \emph{scale-general}: it is offered as applicable to simple things,
composite systems, and the totality, with different principles possible at
different scales.

Scale-generality is enough to generate the trilemma. The approach does not
guarantee that its dynamical selection procedure picks out a proper scale. Brading
says genuine subsystems are ``as picked out by the dynamical laws''
\citep[p.~18]{Brading2015}, and she associates unity with conservation of total
quantity of motion when a system is isolated \citep[p.~1105]{Brading2013}. That
is a selection procedure. What is missing is a guarantee that the procedure
selects a proper subsystem rather than the maximal unity. The 2013 defeat
condition is exactly the case in which it selects no proper subsystem. The
universe-own-region case is the case in which it selects the maximal unity
\emph{and} proper subsystems; the text does not say which present the maximal
unity has.

The missing premise is therefore not a criterion of existence but a
scale-selection guarantee, or an account of the maximal unity's present. A
presentist who wants the global restriction must add a privileged actuality, a
co-presence relation, or a relative existence predicate. Each is a further
structure with a cost; none is P1\textsuperscript{**}.

Relative existence is developed by Balaguer's R-presentism and Mason's domain-
and level-dependent empirical presentism \citep{Balaguer2026,Mason2026}, and
Rovelli's ``neither presentism nor eternalism'' defends a local, unoriented
becoming \citep{Rovelli2019}. These are coherent positions, but they change the
question: every event exists for some system or frame, and the global claim is
given up. They are not restorations of P1\textsuperscript{**}.

\section{Relation to Savitt, Saunders and Pooley}\label{sec:savitt}

Savitt's 2021 objection is distinct and remains unanswered. He argues that
Brading's local present is ``a characterization of a relativistic present, not
relativistic presentism,'' because every dynamical system has a sustaining
region and ``it is unclear under what conditions two dynamical systems share a
present'' \citep[\S3.3]{Savitt2021}. That is a population and co-presence
objection: many presents, no public present. It is not the universe trilemma.
Savitt does not discuss Brading 2015, P1\textsuperscript{**}, or the
law-constitutive principle's application to the universe. The two arguments are
compatible: Savitt gives the semantic verdict, and the trilemma gives the
metaphysical pressure internal to Brading's system.

Saunders's requirement that the presentist provide an invariant, definable
equivalence relation of co-presence is the sharpest version of the co-presence
cost \citep{Saunders2000}; the definability results make a non-trivial symmetric
equivalence relation of a present hard to come by
\citep{CliftonHogarth1995}. It supports Savitt's objection, not the trilemma.
Pooley's distinction between a deflationary analysis of passage compatible with
the block and a privileged global present that does not take relativity
seriously \citep{Pooley2013} is about passage, not about the domain of existence.
Brading's paper is about existence, so Pooley's dilemma does not apply directly.
The trilemma does not claim that local presents fail to account for passage.

The interaction passage is sometimes read as a system-relative existence claim.
Brading writes: ``we belong to the same world as whatever we interact with, and
the rest of that world is as real as we are,'' and asks, ``Does my son exist
relative to me when he is in London and I am here? Of course''
\citep[p.~1110]{Brading2013}. The passage is better read as a single-domain
claim: ``the rest of that world is as real as we are'' is not indexed to a
system, and the following question is about the \emph{determinacy} of what the
son is doing, not about his existence. It does not supply a relativized
quantifier, and it does not answer the trilemma.

\section{Objections and limits}\label{sec:limits}

\paragraph{The identification is a premise, not a theorem.} The note grants
Brading's identification of what exists with the genuine unities. It does not
argue that the identification is true, and it does not need to: the question is
what follows for the universe if the identification is granted. A reader who
rejects the identification is outside the argument. A reader who accepts it must
still decide whether the universe satisfies the unity condition; that is the
interpretive premise discussed in \S\ref{sec:unity}.

\paragraph{The universe's present may be a proper global slice.} This is the
strongest reply to horn (1). A Cauchy slice is a proper region and determines
the evolution of a closed system. If Brading accepts a global slice for the
universe alone, horn (2) holds, and the trilemma's cost is a qualification of
her rejection of the spatially global present, not a contradiction. The note
does not claim that horn (1) is forced. It claims that the text does not settle
which horn holds, and that each horn has a cost.

\paragraph{The local-patch horn.} A local patch whose laws determine the rest
would make (a) true of the universe. The note does not claim that no such
account can be given. It claims that Brading does not give one, and that the
aggregation condition makes the account non-trivial: the patch must sustain the
unity of a whole whose members are spread through the history.

\paragraph{Priority.} The note does not claim that the universe trilemma is
wholly new, and it does not claim that no one has noticed the tension. It claims
that the trilemma is generated by Brading's texts, that it is distinct from
Savitt's objection, and that it has not been answered. A published statement of
the same trilemma would defeat the priority claim, not the argument.

\paragraph{What the note does not show.} It does not show that presentism is
false, that eternalism is true, or that Brading's physics is wrong. It does not
show that the universe's present is $\W$, or that there are no genuine
subsystems. It shows that P1\textsuperscript{**} is false of the universe on
the whole-history horn, true only with an unspecified proper-present account on
the proper-region horns, or restricted on the quantifier horn; and that the
subsystem clauses do not secure a proper union.

\section{Conclusion}

Brading's P1\textsuperscript{**} is her strongest anti-block formulation. It is
also, by her own principle, under pressure from the universe. What exists are
the genuine unities; the approach applies all the way up to the material
universe; the universe is a candidate genuine unity and a thing that exists. Its
present is either the whole history, a proper region (spatially global or
local), or outside the domain. The first makes (a) false of the universe; the
global sub-case needs a qualification of Brading's rejection of the global
present; the local sub-case needs an account of how a local region sustains the
whole; the third gives up the global claim. The trilemma is not a refutation,
but it is a standing problem for the sufficiency sentence.

The 2013 defeat condition and the 2015 survival condition treat subsystem
existence as the decisive empirical question. The universe case is independent
of it. Even if there are genuine subsystems, the universe remains a candidate
genuine unity with a present to fix. The empirical question decides between the
universal and subsystem-only scenarios; it does not decide the universe-own-region
scenario.

The retreat to subsystems gives up the global claim, and even for subsystems
(a) and (b) do not imply a proper union. The missing premise is a
scale-selection guarantee, or an account of the maximal unity's present, not
another bound on the extent of presents. A presentist who wants the global
restriction must add a privileged actuality, a co-presence relation, or a
relative existence predicate. Each is a further structure with a cost; none is
P1\textsuperscript{**}.
