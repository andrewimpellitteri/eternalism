\title{The universe as a dynamical system:\\ a problem of time for Brading's presentism}
\author{Working manuscript}
\date{21 October 2026}
\begin{document}
\maketitle

\begin{abstract}
Brading's law-constitutive presentism locates the present of each thing in the
spatiotemporal region needed to sustain it as a dynamical system, and she says
that the principle of unity behind it applies ``all the way up to the material
universe as a whole.'' Her P1\textsuperscript{**} requires every existing
thing's present to be less than the whole history. This note asks what that
requires of the universe itself. Within special relativity the universe's
present is the whole history, a proper region whose cost Brading has not
discussed, or absent; or the universe is outside the quantifier's domain. In
general relativity the question becomes a version of the problem of time. For
a spatially closed universe, the canonical options are: an internal time whose
reduced Hamiltonian is time dependent, so that the universe fails Brading's
conservation-based test of unity; an ideal clock field that restores a
conserved Hamiltonian but, on Thiemann's construction, requires phantom matter
and predicts departures from standard cosmology; cosmic time along the Hubble
flow, which keeps standard cosmology but is fixed by an idealised model and a
coordinative definition rather than by a sustaining system; or no time at all,
in which case the universe has no present and P1\textsuperscript{**} must be
restricted. Brading's local view avoids the Lorentz-symmetry objection that
Wüthrich raises against Monton's foliation presentism, but it meets the
problem of time at the universe. The result is conditional and does not refute
presentism. It relocates the empirical exposure of Brading's hypothesis: the
decisive question is not only whether there are genuine subsystems, but what
the universe's own present is.
\end{abstract}

\section{The texts and the question}

Katherine Brading's \emph{Presentism as an Empirical Hypothesis} and
\emph{Physically Locating the Present} offer one of the most careful attempts to
keep a presentist ontology alive in relativistic physics
\citep{Brading2013,Brading2015}. She rejects the assumption that spacetime is the
ontological principle of unity of what exists, and replaces it with a
law-constitutive principle: the dynamical laws constitute genuine unities, and
the present of a system is the region needed to sustain its dynamics:

\begin{quote}
The present is a spatiotemporal region of whatever size is necessary to sustain
the dynamical system in question \citep[p.~1109]{Brading2013}.
\end{quote}

The 2015 paper states the position as P1\textsuperscript{**}:

\begin{quote}
(P1\textsuperscript{**}) For each and every thing that exists, that thing
exists only presently, where for each thing the spatiotemporal extent of the
present for that thing is dependent on the dynamics and is (a) less than the
entire spatiotemporal evolution of the universe as represented in Minkowski
spacetime, and (b) less than the entire spatiotemporal evolution of that thing
as represented in Minkowski spacetime \citep[p.~17]{Brading2015}.
\end{quote}

She adds: ``In order to avoid the block universe, all we need is (a)''
\citep[p.~17]{Brading2015}. And she places the decisive empirical question
elsewhere, in the subsystems: whether there are genuine subsystems ``smaller in
spatiotemporal extent than the entirety of spacetime'' is a matter of the
details of the dynamics, and ``only if there are such can the version of
presentism offered here survive'' \citep[p.~18]{Brading2015}.

The scope of the principle is wider than the subsystems. Brading writes that
the law-constitutive approach gives ``a principle of ontological unity that
applies across the board to simple things, composite systems, and component
parts of those systems, and which applies all the way up to the material
universe as a whole. Insofar as what exists is what is one, the
law-constitutive approach to unity is a metaphysical thesis about what exists''
\citep[p.~16]{Brading2015}. This note asks what P1\textsuperscript{**} requires
of the universe itself, first within special relativity, the setting of
Brading's texts, and then in general relativity, where the universe is
standardly treated as a dynamical system in its own right.

The contribution is a bridge, not a new objection family. Savitt has already
objected that every dynamical system has a sustaining region and that it is
unclear when two systems share a present \citep[\S3.3]{Savitt2021}. Monton and
Wüthrich have debated whether presentism can live in quantum gravity through a
preferred foliation \citep{Monton2006,Wuthrich2010}. Isham, Thiemann, and
Bamonti and Thébault have analysed time for the universe in canonical gravity
and cosmology \citep{Isham1991,Thiemann2006,BamontiThebault2025}. None of these
discusses Brading's law-constitutive presentism or the universe's own present.
The claim here is that, applied to the totality, Brading's own unity criterion
meets the problem of time, and that the ways out each carry a definite cost.

\section{The universe as a genuine unity}\label{sec:unity}

Brading's working premise is that ``what exists (what is real) are the genuine
unities,'' and that ``what is `present' depends on what is real (i.e. what
exists, the genuine unities), and what is real depends on the dynamical laws''
\citep[p.~17]{Brading2015}. This note grants the premise. The question is what
follows for the universe if it is granted.

Two further claims are needed for the universe to be an existing thing. The
first is that the universe is a genuine unity. The scope claim above gives the
principle's intended reach, not a verdict; a principle of unity can apply to a
collection and find that it is not one. The textual support for the verdict is
elsewhere: ``The dynamics grounds the unity of what there is, both of the parts
and of the whole (consisting of interacting parts)''
\citep[p.~1110]{Brading2013}; and the 2013 footnote says the law-constitutive
approach covers ``the unity of simple things, composite things, and the
totality of existing things,'' while adding that ``different principles of unity
might be used for the different cases'' \citep[p.~1105, n.~3]{Brading2013}.

The second claim concerns the test. Brading associates unity with the
conservation of total quantity of motion when a system is isolated from
external interactions \citep[p.~1105]{Brading2013}, and with a composite whole
that conserves its total quantity and direction of motion ``when that system as
a whole is free from external interactions'' \citep[p.~16]{Brading2015}. The
universe has no external interactions. In special relativity, a closed
distribution of matter has a conserved total energy-momentum, so the universe
passes the test. That is our application of her criterion, not her statement,
and a reader may resist applying the isolation condition to the totality. If
the premise is rejected, the universe falls outside the domain (\S\ref{sec:sr},
option 3).

For the general-relativistic discussion we need a regimentation of the test.

\begin{definition}[Conservation test, regimented]\label{def:test}
A system passes the conservation test relative to a time parameter $\tau$ if
its evolution in $\tau$ is generated by a quantity that is conserved along that
evolution, that is, by a Hamiltonian with no explicit $\tau$ dependence.
\end{definition}

This is a bridge premise. It extends Brading's Newtonian and special-relativistic
criterion to a setting she does not discuss, and it is the most contestable
step in the argument (\S\ref{sec:limits}).

\section{The universe's present in special relativity}\label{sec:sr}

Grant that the universe is a genuine unity and an existing thing. P1\textsuperscript{**}
is universal: ``for each and every thing that exists''
\citep[p.~17]{Brading2015}. Write $\W$ for the whole history in the common
Minkowski representation. Four cases are exhaustive. Following Brading, ``local''
means a proper part of the block \citep[p.~17]{Brading2015}; below, (2) is split
by whether the proper region is spatially global.

\paragraph{(1) The present is $\W$.} If the region needed to sustain the universe
is its entire evolution, clause (a) is false of the universe, and
P1\textsuperscript{**} is false on its unrestricted reading. Brading herself says
that if a system's needed region ``is the entire spatiotemporal history of the
universe,'' then ``we are back to the block universe'' \citep[p.~17]{Brading2015}.

\paragraph{(2) The present is a proper region.} \emph{(2a) Spatially global.}
If ``needed to sustain'' means ``the data needed to determine the evolution,''
a Cauchy slice or a thin global slab would do. The cost is Brading's rejection of
a global present: ``the idea of a spatially global present common to all
systems is not preserved in this version of presentism, but the rejection of a
spatially global present is one of the central lessons of special relativity''
\citep[p.~17]{Brading2015}. The qualifier ``common to all systems'' leaves room
for a global present for the universe alone. In Minkowski spacetime the
dynamics can even select one: a closed system with timelike total
energy-momentum has a rest frame. But the result is a spatially global present,
in tension with the second clause of the passage, and Brading does not say how
it relates to the local presents of the subsystems. \emph{(2b) Local.} A patch might sustain the universe's unity if the
laws determine the rest from it, but on Brading's account of aggregation ``the
members must stand in relations to one another, and those relations must be
necessary and sufficient for the collection to form a unity''
\citep[p.~16]{Brading2015}. A local region must then encode the relations among
all members; the text does not say how.

\paragraph{(3) The universe is outside the domain.} Either (3a) it is not a
genuine unity, and so, given the premise, does not exist, contrary to the scope
claim; or (3b) ``thing'' is restricted to proper unities, and the quantifier no
longer covers the whole of what exists. In both cases the view becomes a
thesis about proper subsystems.

\paragraph{(4) The universe has no present.} Brading's present is the region
needed to sustain a \emph{dynamical system}. If the universe is a unity but not
a dynamical system in her sense, it has no sustaining region, and
P1\textsuperscript{**} is false or undefined for it. Brading's own defeat
condition speaks against this option being free: it describes the scenario in
which ``the only dynamical system that there is, is this entirety''
\citep[p.~1109]{Brading2013}, so it treats the totality as a dynamical system
with a sustaining region.

Within special relativity, option (2a) is the natural home for a defender, and
its cost is a global present for the universe. In special relativity the
rest frame of the total energy-momentum supplies the slice. In general
relativity there is no such frame for a closed universe, and the choice of
slice becomes a specific physical problem.

\section{The universe's present in general relativity}\label{sec:gr}

In canonical general relativity the Hamiltonian of a spatially closed universe
is a sum of constraints and vanishes on solutions; there is no external time,
and ``nothing seems to happen'' until a time is extracted from the variables
themselves \citep[\S1]{Thiemann2006}. A spatially global present for the universe,
option (2a), is then a region bounded by leaves of some global time function.
Which function? Isham distinguishes two broad strategies: reduce the theory to
true canonical form by choosing a time before quantising, or quantise all
variables and impose the constraints, leading to the Wheeler--DeWitt equation
\citep[pp.~196--198]{Isham1991}. Combining these with the cosmological
literature gives four routes for the universe's present. The question for each
is whether the universe, so timed, passes the conservation test.

\paragraph{(a) An internal time.} Choose a time variable from the gravitational
or matter degrees of freedom and solve the constraint for its conjugate
momentum, which becomes the reduced Hamiltonian. In a closed
Friedmann--Lemaître--Robertson--Walker minisuperspace Isham does this for the
radius of the universe, for its conjugate momentum, and for a scalar field, and
lists among the general problems that ``the reduced Hamiltonian is time
dependent'' \citep[pp.~199--200]{Isham1991}. He comments:

\begin{quote}
A time-dependent Hamiltonian means that energy can be fed into, or taken out of,
the quantum system. In a normal physical situation this arises because the
system is not closed, and then the time-dependence of the Hamiltonian is
determined by the environment. However, a compact three-manifold $\Sigma$ (the
``universe'') has no external environment, and so the time-dependence is perhaps
a little odd \citep[p.~200]{Isham1991}.
\end{quote}

Wüthrich reports the same point against the constant-mean-curvature route to a
presentist foliation \citep[p.~274]{Wuthrich2010}. For Brading the oddity is
more than odd. A universe whose evolution in its own global time is generated by
a time-dependent Hamiltonian fails the conservation test of
Definition~\ref{def:test}. It has a present, but by her criterion it is not the
kind of isolated whole that conserves its total quantity of motion. Isham adds a
second cost: ``There is no natural choice for the time variable and no reason to
suppose that different choices will give equivalent quantum theories''
\citep[p.~200]{Isham1991}.

\paragraph{(b) An ideal clock.} The time dependence is not universal. In Isham's
own scalar-field example the reduced Hamiltonian,
$H_\phi=\pm\bigl(a^2\pi_a^2/12+12a^4-2a^6V(t)\bigr)^{1/2}$ \citep[eq.~5.2.8]{Isham1991},
has no explicit time dependence when the potential $V$ vanishes. Thiemann
generalises the point: if the Lagrangian of a scalar field coupled to general
relativity contains only derivatives, the theory deparametrises completely, and
there is ``a distinguished notion of time generated by an invariant, positive and
time independent physical Hamiltonian'' \citep[p.~20]{Thiemann2006}. On this
route the universe has a global time and passes the conservation test.

The cost is physical. To keep the physical Hamiltonian close to that of
standard cosmology the clock Lagrangian must be of Dirac--Born--Infeld type,
which for small gradients is a massless field ``with the wrong sign, i.e. it is
a phantom,'' and ``non-trivial potentials are forbidden''
\citep[p.~5]{Thiemann2006}. The phantom's negative energy must be compensated by
other matter, and the concrete model implies that the universe recollapses
\citep[pp.~9--10]{Thiemann2006}. Above all, Thiemann claims that ``whatever
matter is used for deparametrisation, there will be corrections to the standard
FRW equations. This should have observable consequences!''
\citep[p.~9]{Thiemann2006}. Bamonti and Thébault accept that this route changes
the empirical content of cosmology \citep[\S5]{BamontiThebault2025}. On route (b),
then, a dynamically privileged present for the universe that keeps its unity is
an empirical bet on new matter with a cosmological signature.

\paragraph{(c) Cosmic time.} Bamonti and Thébault defend a conservative
alternative: read the time in the Friedmann equations as proper time along the
Hubble flow, in synchronous gauge, so that the equations describe the
correlation between two partial observables, the Hubble parameter and cosmic
time \citep[\S\S4--5]{BamontiThebault2025}. The cost is that ``there is no
physical system which even approximately measures proper time along the Hubble
flow'' \citep[p.~5]{BamontiThebault2025}. Cosmic time is recovered as a
model-based global average, with the clock hypothesis acting as a coordinative
definition in Reichenbach's sense \citep[pp.~19--20]{BamontiThebault2025}. They
do not address whether the universe passes a conservation test in this gauge,
and nothing here is a criticism of their proposal. The point is narrower. If
the universe's present is a slice of constant cosmic time, it is fixed by an
idealised homogeneous model and a coordinative definition. It is not ``the
region necessary to sustain the dynamical system'' in Brading's sense, since no
actual system's dynamics singles it out.

\paragraph{(d) No time.} If no time is extracted, the constraints are imposed on
the states and the universe is described by a solution of the Wheeler--DeWitt
equation with no evolution parameter \citep[\S5.3]{Isham1991}. The universe may
then count as a conserved whole, but it has no present. This is option (4) of
\S\ref{sec:sr}, now physically motivated, and P1\textsuperscript{**} must be
restricted to subsystems with internal clocks.

The argument has the following structure. Grant that the universe is a genuine
unity and a thing (\S\ref{sec:unity}), and the regimented test
(Definition~\ref{def:test}). Then, for a spatially closed universe in canonical
general relativity, either the universe has no present (d), or its present is a
global slice fixed by a choice of time, and each choice examined costs
something Brading's view needs: the universe's unity (a), standard cosmology
(b), or the dynamical fixing of the present (c). The note does not claim the
four routes are exhaustive of all possible physics, only that they are the
routes in the cited literature and that the text gives the defender none of
them for free.

\section{Monton, Wüthrich and the local present}\label{sec:mw}

The debate over presentism in quantum gravity has concerned a global present.
Monton grants that presentism is incompatible with special and general
relativity, and argues that it is compatible with quantum theories of gravity
that use a fixed foliation, such as constant-mean-curvature canonical gravity,
on a theoretical hypothesis that one leaf represents the existing events
\citep{Monton2006}. Wüthrich replies that fixed-foliation theories break a
Lorentz symmetry that is ``fantastically well confirmed,'' and that the
constant-mean-curvature approach is limited in scope and does not by itself
supply a present that underwrites becoming \citep[pp.~269--275]{Wuthrich2010}.

Brading's view is designed to avoid that exchange. It does not posit a
foliation; presents are local, dynamically individuated and not common to all
systems. Wüthrich's classification of presentist responses to special relativity
covers frame-relative existence, the point present and the past-light-cone
present \citep[pp.~263--264]{Wuthrich2010}; it does not cover a present fixed by
a system's dynamics. So the Lorentz-symmetry objection does not apply to
Brading's subsystems.

It applies, in effect, to the universe. Routes (a) to (c) of \S\ref{sec:gr} all
give the universe a global slice, and route (b) is precisely a fixed-foliation
theory of the kind Monton relies on. Brading's local presentism therefore
inherits the Monton--Wüthrich problem at exactly one scale, the scale at which
her principle of unity is said to apply ``all the way up.''

\section{The defeat condition, relocated}\label{sec:defeat}

Brading's 2013 defeat condition describes one scenario:

\begin{quote}
It might turn out that, for whatever system we try to consider, the size of the
spatiotemporal region necessary to sustain it is the entire history of the
universe, encompassing all that ever has been and ever will be. In other words,
the only dynamical system that there is, is this entirety, and there are no
genuine subsystems of the universe. If that turns out to be the case, then this
version of presentism is defeated and the block universe triumphs
\citep[p.~1109]{Brading2013}.
\end{quote}

The universe case is independent of it. Even if there are genuine subsystems
with proper presents, the universe remains a candidate unity with a present to
fix. Brading's empirical question, whether there are genuine subsystems, does not
decide what that present is. Section~\ref{sec:gr} gives the universe case its own
empirical exposure. On route (b) a dynamically privileged present for the
universe that preserves its unity is tied to matter that, on Thiemann's
analysis, alters late-time cosmology; on route (c) standard cosmology is kept and
the present is fixed by convention; on routes (a) and (d) the universe loses its
unity or its present. Brading calls her view an empirical hypothesis. Applied to
the totality, its exposure is cosmological.

\section{The retreat to subsystems and the coverage model}\label{sec:coverage}

The natural reply is to restrict P1\textsuperscript{**} to proper subsystems
(option 3b, or route (d)). The restricted view no longer says that every
existing thing exists only presently. A defender who instead keeps the global
claim and tries to secure it through clauses (a) and (b) for the subsystems
faces a further, independent problem: the clauses do not imply that any event
lies outside every present.

\begin{proposition}[(a) and (b) do not imply a proper union]\label{prop:two}
There is a model in which (a) and (b) hold for every system and
$\bigcup_i\U_i=\W$.
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}
Let $\W=\{a,b,c,d\}$. Let system 1 have history $\Hh_1=\{a,b,c\}$ and present
$\U_1=\{a,b\}$, and system 2 have history $\Hh_2=\{b,c,d\}$ and present
$\U_2=\{c,d\}$. Then $\U_i\subsetneq\W$ and $\U_i\subsetneq\Hh_i$ for $i=1,2$,
but $\U_1\cup\U_2=\W$.
\end{proof}

The model is set-theoretic, not dynamical; it regiments ``less than'' as proper
inclusion; and it uses a common event domain $\W$. Brading's 2013 note 7 denies
determinate ontological spatial or temporal relations between systems: ``All the
ontology is carried by the dynamics, and we frame the dynamics spatiotemporally,
but ontologically, the dynamics requires no such spatiotemporal underpinning''
\citep[p.~1109, n.~7]{Brading2013}. The model therefore treats $\W$ as the common
epistemic representation in which the comparison is stated. With those
assumptions, per-system properness is coverage-neutral, a formal counterpart of
Savitt's observation that every dynamical system has a sustaining region
\citep[\S3.3]{Savitt2021}.

\section{Objections and limits}\label{sec:limits}

\paragraph{Brading's view is special-relativistic.} Her texts work in Minkowski
spacetime, and P1\textsuperscript{**} is stated there. Section~\ref{sec:sr} is
internal to that setting. Section~\ref{sec:gr} is an extension, and its bridge
premise, Definition~\ref{def:test}, is ours. A defender may reject it, for
instance by holding that the universe's unity does not require a conserved
generator of its evolution. That is a substantive revision of the criterion
Brading takes from Newton, and the note's claim is that some such revision, or
one of the costs in the table, is required.

\paragraph{The routes may not be exhaustive.} The note claims only that the
routes in the cited literature each carry a cost, not that no other route exists.

\paragraph{Scope of the physics.} Isham's examples are closed minisuperspace
models; Thiemann's concrete model is spatially flat; and his claim that every
deparametrising matter field yields corrections is asserted in the passage
cited, not proved for all matter. Bamonti and Thébault do not discuss
conservation in synchronous gauge. Each of these is a point at which the
argument could be weakened by further physics.

\paragraph{Universe and representation.} Brading's note 7 suggests that the
spatiotemporal representation carries no ontology of its own. If so, the global
slice of routes (a) to (c) is a representational device, and the universe's
present is not a region at all. Then route (d) or option (4) holds, and the
restriction of P1\textsuperscript{**} follows.

\paragraph{Priority.} No published statement of this argument was found in
the sources read: Brading 2013 and 2015, Savitt 2021, Monton 2006 (2005
preprint), Wüthrich 2010, Isham 1991 (pp.~192--202), Thiemann 2006 and Bamonti
and Thébault 2025, all at full-text depth; Balaguer's \emph{How to Be a
Presentist} \citep{Balaguer2026} and Mason's empirical presentism
\citep{Mason2026} only at abstract depth. Balaguer's chapter on special
relativity is the main unread risk. A prior statement would defeat the priority
claim, not the argument.

\paragraph{What the note does not show.} It does not show that presentism is
false, that eternalism is true, or that Brading's subsystem presents are
incoherent. It does not show that the universe's present is $\W$, and it takes
no position on the cosmology of routes (b) and (c).

\section{Conclusion}

Brading's principle of unity is offered for the whole of what exists, up to the
material universe. Taken at that scale, P1\textsuperscript{**} needs an account
of the universe's own present. In special relativity the options are the whole
history, a global slice of the kind she elsewhere rejects, an unexplained local region, exclusion from
the domain, or no present at all. In general relativity the global-slice option
becomes the problem of time. The universe can have a present and fail the
conservation test (internal time), have a present and pass it at the price of
new matter with cosmological consequences (ideal clock), have a present fixed by
an idealised model and a coordinative definition (cosmic time), or have no
present and leave P1\textsuperscript{**} restricted to subsystems. Brading's
local presentism escapes the Lorentz-symmetry objection to foliation presentism
for every system except the universe. Her hypothesis remains empirical, but its
exposure is not only the existence of subsystems; it is also the cosmology of the
whole.
