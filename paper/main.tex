\title{The universe as a thing:\\ Brading's P1\textsuperscript{**} and the limits of local presentism}
\author{Working manuscript}
\date{21 October 2026}
\begin{document}
\maketitle

\begin{abstract}
Brading's local present is a spatiotemporal region of whatever size is needed to
sustain a dynamical system. Her P1\textsuperscript{**} requires every existing
thing's present to be less than the whole history, and she says that ``in order
to avoid the block universe, all we need is (a).'' This note argues that her own
principle makes the argument fail for the universe itself. Brading holds that
what exists are the genuine unities, and that the law-constitutive principle
applies ``all the way up to the material universe as a whole.'' The universe is
therefore a genuine unity and a thing that exists; its sustaining region is the
whole history; so clause (a) is false of it. The alternatives---a spatially
global present, a local or point present, or the universe's non-existence---are
each excluded by Brading's text. The 2013 defeat condition does not cover the
case that matters: the universe can have the whole history as its region while
genuine subsystems have proper regions. The empirical question about subsystems
is therefore not the question on which the view turns. A second argument shows that even for proper
subsystems, (a) and (b) do not imply a proper union; a two-system, four-event
model satisfies both and covers the history. The missing premise is not another
bound on the extent of presents but a selection of which unities count---a
selection Brading's scale-free principle cannot make. The verdict is limited:
P1\textsuperscript{**} is either false of the universe or restricted in a way
that gives up the global presentist claim.
\end{abstract}

\section{The texts and the tension}

Katherine Brading's \emph{Presentism as an Empirical Hypothesis} and
\emph{Physically Locating the Present} offer one of the most careful attempts to
keep a presentist ontology alive within special relativity
\citep{Brading2013,Brading2015}. She rejects the assumption that spacetime is the
ontological principle of unity of what exists, and replaces it with a
law-constitutive principle: the dynamical laws constitute genuine unities, and
the present of a system is the spatiotemporal region needed to sustain its
dynamics. The 2013 paper introduces the idea in a compact form:

\begin{quote}
The present is a spatiotemporal region of whatever size is necessary to sustain
the dynamical system in question \citep[p.~1109]{Brading2013}.
\end{quote}

The size is left open. It ``might turn out to be much, much smaller than the
abstract spatiotemporal structure within which the evolution of that unity is
completely described, or it might be the size of the block universe''
\citep[p.~1109]{Brading2013}. Brading then assumes genuine subsystems and draws
the local conclusion:

\begin{quote}
So let us begin from the position that there are genuine subsystems. This means
that the size of the spatiotemporal region required to sustain the system is
less than the entire block universe, and so the present is (at least in the
first instance) local, not global \citep[p.~1110]{Brading2013}.
\end{quote}

The 2015 paper states the position as P1\textsuperscript{**}:

\begin{quote}
(P1\textsuperscript{**}) For each and every thing that exists, that thing
exists only presently, where for each thing the spatiotemporal extent of the
present for that thing is dependent on the dynamics and is (a) less than the
entire spatiotemporal evolution of the universe as represented in Minkowski
spacetime, and (b) less than the entire spatiotemporal evolution of that thing
as represented in Minkowski spacetime \citep[p.~17]{Brading2015}.
\end{quote}

She adds: ``In order to avoid the block universe, all we need is (a)''
\citep[p.~17]{Brading2015}. Her own statement of the effect is weaker: a
smaller-than-universe present ``calls the ontology of a block universe into
question'' \citep[p.~17]{Brading2015}. And she leaves the empirical question
open: whether there are genuine subsystems ``smaller in spatiotemporal extent
than the entirety of spacetime'' is a matter of the details of the dynamics, and
``only if there are such can the version of presentism offered here survive''
\citep[p.~18]{Brading2015}.

The tension is internal. On the same pages Brading commits herself to a
principle that makes the material universe a genuine unity and hence a thing
that exists. P1\textsuperscript{**} then applies to the universe itself. But the
region needed to sustain the universe is the whole history, so clause (a) is
false of it. The rest of this note states the argument, answers the obvious
replies, and draws the limited consequence.

The note does not claim that the coverage problem is wholly new, and it does not
compete with Savitt's 2021 objection. It claims that the totality argument is
distinct from Savitt's, that it is generated by Brading's own texts, and that
P1\textsuperscript{**} cannot be true of the universe as a whole. The formal
model in \S\ref{sec:coverage} is a second, independent problem for the subsystem
family.

\section{What exists are the genuine unities}\label{sec:unity}

Brading's law-constitutive account is not only an account of the unity of
ordinary objects. She says that the principle applies across the board:

\begin{quote}
We are being offered a principle of ontological unity that applies across the
board to simple things, composite systems, and component parts of those
systems, and which applies all the way up to the material universe as a whole.
Insofar as what exists is what is one, the law-constitutive approach to unity is
a metaphysical thesis about what exists \citep[p.~16]{Brading2015}.
\end{quote}

The ``insofar as'' might look like a hedge, but the surrounding text removes the
doubt. Brading says that she begins ``from the premise that what exists (what is
real) are the genuine unities,'' and that ``what is `present' depends on what is
real (i.e. what exists, the genuine unities), and what is real depends on the
dynamical laws'' \citep[p.~17]{Brading2015}. This is not a conditional aside; it
is the order of priority that distinguishes her view from the traditional
presentism she rejects. She first determines what is real via the
law-constitutive approach, and then seeks features of what is real as the ground
of what is present.

The universe is the paradigm case of the principle's scope. Brading says the
principle applies ``all the way up to the material universe as a whole''
\citep[p.~16]{Brading2015}, and her 2013 note says the law-constitutive approach
covers ``the unity of simple things, composite things, and the totality of
existing things'' \citep[p.~1106, n.~3]{Brading2013}. The universe is not a
collection of genuine unities that falls outside the principle; it is the
maximal case to which the principle is meant to apply.

Two consequences follow. First, the universe is a genuine unity. Second, by
Brading's identification of what exists with the genuine unities, the universe
is a thing that exists. It is not merely a set or a collection; it is the
maximal unified thing. This is the premise that the rest of the argument uses.

The identification is also what makes the view a metaphysical thesis rather
than a taxonomy of dynamical systems. If existence were not unity, the
law-constitutive approach would tell us which systems are unified but not which
of them are real. Brading's claim is stronger: the unified systems just are what
there is. A reader who rejects the identification is not yet engaged with her
view; a reader who accepts it must follow it to the universe.

\section{The reductio}\label{sec:reductio}

The argument has four premises, each sourced to Brading's text.

\begin{enumerate}
\item[(T1)] What exists are the genuine unities, and the law-constitutive
principle applies to the material universe as a whole
\citep[p.~16]{Brading2015}.
\item[(T2)] The present of a thing is the spatiotemporal region necessary to
sustain it \citep[pp.~1109--1110]{Brading2013}; \citep[p.~17]{Brading2015}.
\item[(T3)] P1\textsuperscript{**} requires, for every existing thing, that
this region be less than the entire spatiotemporal evolution of the universe
\citep[p.~17]{Brading2015}.
\item[(T4)] The region necessary to sustain the universe as a whole is the
entire history. The universe's unity is constituted by the relations among its
members \citep[p.~16]{Brading2015}, and its members are spread through the
history; a region that omitted any of them would omit part of what the universe
is.
\end{enumerate}

From (T1) and (T2), the universe is a genuine unity and has a present: the region
necessary to sustain it. From (T3), that region must be less than the whole
history $\W$. From (T4), it is $\W$. Contradiction. Therefore
P1\textsuperscript{**} (a) is false of the universe, and
P1\textsuperscript{**}, which quantifies over every thing that exists, is false.

The argument can be put as a dilemma about the universe's present. It is either
$\W$, or a proper region. If it is $\W$, (a) is false. If it is proper, it is
either spatially global or local. A spatially global present is rejected by
Brading: ``the idea of a spatially global present common to all systems is not
preserved in this version of presentism, but the rejection of a spatially global
present is one of the central lessons of special relativity''
\citep[p.~17]{Brading2015}. A local or point-like present is the position she
associates with Stein's ``peculiarly extreme (but pluralistic!) form of
solipsism'' \citep[p.~1102]{Brading2013}, and she says her view avoids it
\citep[pp.~1109--1110]{Brading2013}. A proper region that is neither global nor
local would have to sustain the universe's unity while omitting some of the
relations among its members; that is not a region sufficient to sustain the
unity, on Brading's own account of aggregation: ``The members must stand in
relations to one another, and those relations must be necessary and sufficient
for the collection to form a unity'' \citep[p.~16]{Brading2015}.

The remaining escape is to deny that the universe is a thing that exists. That
contradicts (T1). It also contradicts the order of priority Brading defends:
what is real are the genuine unities, and the universe is the maximal one. If
the universe is not a thing, the principle does not apply to the case that most
obviously satisfies it.

The conclusion is not that Brading's physics is wrong, or that presentism is
false. It is that P1\textsuperscript{**} is not a condition that the universe
can satisfy. The one thing whose existence is entailed by the law-constitutive
principle is the one thing for which clause (a) fails. ``All we need is (a)''
cannot be satisfied by the universe.

\section{The defeat condition is incomplete}\label{sec:defeat}

Brading's 2013 defeat condition makes the same point in a different form. She
writes:

\begin{quote}
It might turn out that, for whatever system we try to consider, the size of the
spatiotemporal region necessary to sustain it is the entire history of the
universe, encompassing all that ever has been and ever will be. In other words,
the only dynamical system that there is, is this entirety, and there are no
genuine subsystems of the universe. If that turns out to be the case, then this
version of presentism is defeated and the block universe triumphs
\citep[p.~1109]{Brading2013}.
\end{quote}

The passage addresses one scenario: every system we consider has the whole
history as its sustaining region. In that scenario there are no proper
subsystems. But it is not the only scenario in which the universe's region is
$\W$. The universe can have $\W$ as its region while its subsystems have proper
regions. The subsystems are parts of the universe, not alternatives to it.
Brading's principle makes the universe a genuine unity; its region is $\W$; the
subsystems can be genuine too. The defeat condition does not cover this case.

This is not a verbal slip. It is the reason the 2015 survival condition is
incomplete. Brading says that ``only if there are such [genuine subsystems
smaller than the entirety of spacetime] can the version of presentism offered
here survive'' \citep[p.~18]{Brading2015}. But the existence of such subsystems
does not make P1\textsuperscript{**} true of the universe. The universe remains a
genuine unity with the whole history as its present. The survival condition is
necessary for the local presents of subsystems; it is not sufficient for the
truth of P1\textsuperscript{**}, because P1\textsuperscript{**} also applies to
the universe.

The empirical question is therefore not the question on which the view turns.
Brading frames the issue as whether there are genuine subsystems, and treats the
answer as a matter for the details of physics. The totality argument says that
the answer does not decide the fate of P1\textsuperscript{**}: the universe is a
counterexample whether or not subsystems exist. The empirical question may
decide whether there are local presents for subsystems; it does not decide
whether the universal clause is true.

\section{The retreat to subsystems}\label{sec:coverage}

The natural reply is to restrict P1\textsuperscript{**} to proper subsystems.
The universe is not a ``thing'' in the intended sense; the quantifier ranges over
the genuine unities below the whole. This reply has two costs.

First, it gives up the global claim. P1\textsuperscript{**} says ``for each and
every thing that exists.'' If the universe is excluded, the quantifier is
restricted, and the view no longer says that every existing thing exists only
presently. It says that every proper subsystem does. The universe itself is left
out, and with it the presentist claim that the whole of reality is present. A
restricted quantifier is a different thesis, not a reading of the original.

Second, even for proper subsystems, (a) and (b) do not imply a proper union.
Clause (a) says, for every system $i$,
\[
\U_i\subsetneq\W .
\]
It does not imply
\[
\bigcup_i \U_i\subsetneq\W .
\]

\begin{proposition}[Non-implication]\label{prop:one}
Local boundedness of every $\U_i$ is consistent with
$\bigcup_i\U_i=\W$.
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}
Take $\W=\{a,b,c\}$ and the proper subsets $\{a,b\}$ and $\{b,c\}$. Their union
is $\W$.
\end{proof}

The natural reply is that clause (b) closes the gap. Clause (b) says that each
thing's present is less than the entire spatiotemporal evolution of that thing.
That is a bound on the present relative to the thing's own history. It is not a
bound on the present relative to every other thing's present, and it is not a
claim that some event lies outside every present.

\begin{proposition}[(a) and (b) do not imply a proper union]\label{prop:two}
There is a model in which (a) and (b) hold for every system and
$\bigcup_i\U_i=\W$.
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}
Let $\W=\{a,b,c,d\}$. Let system 1 have history $\Hh_1=\{a,b,c\}$ and present
$\U_1=\{a,b\}$. Let system 2 have history $\Hh_2=\{b,c,d\}$ and present
$\U_2=\{c,d\}$. Then $\U_1\subsetneq\W$ and $\U_2\subsetneq\W$, so (a) holds.
Also $\U_1\subsetneq\Hh_1$ and $\U_2\subsetneq\Hh_2$, so (b) holds. But
$\U_1\cup\U_2=\{a,b,c,d\}=\W$.
\end{proof}

The model is set-theoretic, not dynamical. That is sufficient for the
non-implication: if (a) and (b) are offered as the conditions that exclude the
block, a model that satisfies them and covers the history shows that they do
not, by themselves, exclude it. A defender who says that the model is not
law-constituted must supply the further dynamical condition and show that it
excludes coverage. That is precisely the missing premise, and it is not (a) or
(b).

The failure is structural. Clause (b) puts events outside their \emph{own}
system's present. It does not put any event outside \emph{every} present. A
defender needs the latter: an event $e\in\W$ such that for all $i$, $e\notin
\U_i$. Neither (a) nor (b) delivers such an event. Per-item properness is
coverage-neutral. Non-coverage requires a joint constraint on the family, or a
selection principle that removes some presents from the family. Brading
supplies neither.

The two problems are independent. The totality argument says that
P1\textsuperscript{**} is false of the universe; the coverage argument says
that, even if the universe is set aside, the subsystem clauses do not secure a
proper union. A defender who restricts the quantifier to avoid the first falls
into the second. A defender who adds a selection principle to avoid the second
still has not answered the first, unless the principle also excludes the
universe---which is the quantifier restriction again.

\section{Why the principle cannot select a scale}\label{sec:scale}

The diagnosis is not that Brading lacks an existence criterion. She has one:
what exists are the genuine unities \citep[p.~17]{Brading2015}. The problem is
that the criterion is scale-free. It applies to simple things, composite
systems, component parts, and the material universe as a whole. A criterion that
confers existence on every level of unity cannot, by itself, select a local
level as the level of existence-conferring presents. It confers existence on the
maximal unity too.

This is the sense in which the missing premise is a principle of
\emph{selection}, not a further bound on the extent of presents. To get the
local presentist result, one needs to say which unities' presents count. The
options are familiar: a privileged actual system, a co-presence relation, or a
frame- or level-relative existence predicate. Each is a further structure, and
each has a cost.

A privileged actual system is a metaphysical posit. It is not derived from the
dynamics, and if the privileged system is the universe, its present is $\W$ and
the view yields the block. A co-presence relation answers Savitt's objection
about shared presents, but it does not answer coverage: a relation can relate
all the local presents while their union is still $\W$. Frame- or level-relative
existence blocks coverage by changing the question; every event exists for some
system or level, and the global presentist claim is given up. Balaguer's
R-presentism and Mason's domain- and level-dependent empirical presentism
develop versions of this route \citep{Balaguer2026,Mason2026}, and Rovelli's
``neither presentism nor eternalism'' defends a local, unoriented becoming
\citep{Rovelli2019}. These are coherent positions, but they are not
P1\textsuperscript{**}.

The scale-free criterion also explains why the 2013 defeat condition is
incomplete. Brading asks whether every system's region is the whole history, and
concludes that there are no proper subsystems. But the criterion applies at
every scale, so the maximal unity and its proper parts can both be genuine. The
region of the whole can be $\W$ while the regions of the parts are proper. The
defeat condition does not consider this case.

\section{Relation to Savitt, Saunders and Pooley}\label{sec:savitt}

Savitt's 2021 objection is distinct and remains unanswered. He argues that
Brading's local present is ``a characterization of a relativistic present, not
relativistic presentism,'' because every dynamical system has a sustaining
region and ``it is unclear under what conditions two dynamical systems share a
present'' \citep[\S3.3]{Savitt2021}. That is a population and co-presence
objection: many presents, no public present. It is not the totality argument.
Savitt does not discuss Brading 2015, P1\textsuperscript{**}, or the
law-constitutive principle's application to the universe. The two arguments are
compatible: Savitt gives the semantic verdict, and the totality argument gives
the metaphysical reason internal to Brading's system.

Saunders's requirement that the presentist provide an invariant, definable
equivalence relation of co-presence is the sharpest version of the co-presence
cost \citep{Saunders2000}; the definability results make a non-trivial symmetric
equivalence relation of a present hard to come by
\citep{CliftonHogarth1995}. It supports Savitt's objection, not the totality
argument. Pooley's distinction between a deflationary analysis of passage
compatible with the block and a privileged global present that does not take
relativity seriously \citep{Pooley2013} is about passage, not about the domain
of existence. Brading's paper is about existence, so Pooley's dilemma does not
apply directly. The totality argument does not claim that local presents fail to
account for passage.

The interaction passage is sometimes read as a system-relative existence claim.
Brading writes: ``we belong to the same world as whatever we interact with, and
the rest of that world is as real as we are,'' and asks, ``Does my son exist
relative to me when he is in London and I am here? Of course''
\citep[p.~1110]{Brading2013}. The passage is better read as a single-domain
claim: ``the rest of that world is as real as we are'' is not indexed to a
system, and the following question is about the \emph{determinacy} of what the
son is doing, not about his existence. It does not supply a relativized
quantifier, and it does not answer the totality argument.

\section{Objections and limits}\label{sec:limits}

\paragraph{The ``insofar as'' hedge.} A reader might say that Brading's
``insofar as what exists is what is one'' is conditional, and that she could
withdraw the identification. The surrounding text makes this difficult. She says
she begins ``from the premise that what exists (what is real) are the genuine
unities,'' and that ``what is `present' depends on what is real (i.e. what
exists, the genuine unities)'' \citep[p.~17]{Brading2015}. The identification is
the order of priority that distinguishes her view from the traditional
presentism she rejects. If she withdraws it, the law-constitutive approach no
longer tells us what exists; it tells us only which systems are unified. That is
a different, weaker view, and it is not the one P1\textsuperscript{**} states.

\paragraph{A proper local sustaining region.} The weakest premise is (T4). A
defender might say that the universe's unity is sustained by a proper region
that is neither a global slice nor a point: a local patch whose laws determine
the rest. If such a region exists, (a) could be true of the universe. The reply
is that the region must contain the relations among the universe's members, and
those members are spread through the history. A local patch that omits them
omits part of what the universe is. The defender needs a principled account of
how a proper, local region sustains the whole; the note does not claim that no
such account can be given. It claims that Brading does not give one, and that
her own rejection of the global slice and the point leaves no obvious candidate.

\paragraph{Priority.} The note does not claim that the totality argument is
wholly new, and it does not claim that no one has noticed the tension. It claims
that the argument is generated by Brading's texts, that it is distinct from
Savitt's, and that it has not been answered. A published statement of the same
inconsistency would defeat the priority claim, not the argument.

\paragraph{What the note does not show.} It does not show that presentism is
false, that eternalism is true, or that Brading's physics is wrong. It does not
show that there are no genuine subsystems. It shows that
P1\textsuperscript{**} is either false of the universe or restricted in a way
that gives up the global claim, and that the subsystem clauses do not secure a
proper union.

\section{Conclusion}

Brading's P1\textsuperscript{**} is her strongest anti-block formulation. It is
also, by her own principle, false of the universe. What exists are the genuine
unities; the principle applies all the way up to the material universe; the
universe is therefore a thing that exists. Its sustaining region is the whole
history. Clause (a) requires that region to be less than the whole. The
contradiction is not a defect of the physics; it is a defect of the formulation.

The 2013 defeat condition and the 2015 survival condition are incomplete. The
universe's region can be the whole history while genuine subsystems have proper
regions, and the existence of subsystems does not make
P1\textsuperscript{**} true of the universe. The empirical question about
subsystems is not the question on which the view turns.

The retreat to subsystems gives up the global claim, and even for subsystems
(a) and (b) do not imply a proper union. The missing premise is a selection of
which unities' presents count, not another bound on the extent of presents.
Brading's principle is scale-free, so it cannot make that selection by itself. A
presentist who wants the global restriction must add a privileged actuality, a
co-presence relation, or a relative existence predicate. Each is a further
structure with a cost; none is P1\textsuperscript{**}.
