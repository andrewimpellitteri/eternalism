\title{Does the Universe Have a Present?\\[2pt]
\large Law-Constitutive Presentism and the Problem of Time}
\author{Working manuscript}
\date{24 October 2026}
\begin{document}
\maketitle

\begin{abstract}
Katherine Brading proposes that the present of each thing is the
spatiotemporal region needed to sustain it as a dynamical system, and that
the law-constitutive principle of unity behind this proposal applies ``all
the way up to the material universe as a whole.'' Her formulation
P1\textsuperscript{**} then requires the universe's own present to be less
than its entire history. I argue that this requirement is where the view is
under most strain. Subsystems face a problem of location: which of their
sustaining regions is present. The universe faces in addition a problem of
shape. It has no outside, so a stage of the universe must be bounded by
global surfaces, and something must determine which ones. In special
relativity the universe's total energy--momentum does this. In a spatially
closed general-relativistic universe nothing in the dynamics does, unless
the universe has what Brading, reading Newton, calls a true time: a time
``unique and proper to the \ldots\ system of bodies in question.'' Whether a
closed universe has such a time is the problem of time. The answers in
canonical gravity and cosmology supply apparent times that disagree, a true
time only for an enlarged system containing posited reference matter, a
mathematical time constructed from an idealized model, or no time at all;
the last remains a live candidate, and constant-mean-curvature time is a
further one if the unity test is read as momentum conservation. Unless the
defender can say which proper regions are the universe's presents, the only
reading of its sustaining region that selects anything gives the whole
history, which by Brading's own criterion returns the block universe.
Restricting P1\textsuperscript{**} to proper subsystems is
coherent, but it leaves a view on which the totality is a four-dimensional
unity. Unlike Gödel's objection to cosmic time, this objection does not turn
on contingency, which Brading's empiricism welcomes.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}\label{sec:intro}

Katherine Brading has proposed a version of presentism designed to take
special relativity seriously without adding a preferred foliation
\citep{Brading2013,Brading2015}. Its central move is to stop treating
spacetime as the principle of ontological unity of what exists. Following
what she reads as Newton's law-constitutive approach to bodies, she takes
the genuine unities to be what exists and lets the dynamical laws say what
the unities are. A thing's present is then ``a spatiotemporal region of
whatever size is necessary to sustain the dynamical system in question''
\citep[p.~1109]{Brading2013}. Presents so understood are local, dynamically
individuated and need not be shared.

The main objection in the literature is Savitt's. He grants that this yields
a relativistic present but observes that ``every dynamical system that has
existed, does exist or will exist had, has or will have a region of whatever
size is necessary to sustain it''; the proposal is ``a characterization of a
relativistic present, not relativistic presentism''
\citep[sec.~3.3]{Savitt2021}. Call this the \emph{location problem}: nothing in
the proposal says which of a system's sustaining regions is the present one.

This paper raises a different problem, which arises at one scale only.
Brading's principle of unity is meant to apply ``all the way up to the
material universe as a whole'' \citep[p.~16]{Brading2015}, and her
formulation P1\textsuperscript{**} requires every existing thing's present to
be ``less than the entire spatiotemporal evolution of the universe''
\citep[p.~17]{Brading2015}. If the universe is a thing, its present must be a
proper part of its history. I argue that the universe faces, in addition to
the location problem, a \emph{shape problem} that subsystems avoid. A stage of
a subsystem is cut out by the subsystem's own world-tube and dynamics. A stage
of the universe has no outside: it must be bounded by global surfaces, and
something must determine which ones. In special relativity the universe's
total energy--momentum does so. In a spatially closed general-relativistic
universe nothing in the dynamics does so, unless the universe has what
Brading herself, reading Newton, calls a \emph{true time}: a time ``unique
and proper to the body (or system of bodies) in question''
\citep[p.~20]{Brading2017}. Whether a closed universe has such a time is the
problem of time. The answers on offer in canonical gravity and cosmology
supply apparent times that disagree, a true time only for an enlarged system
containing posited reference matter, a mathematical time constructed from an
idealized model, or no time at all.

The conclusion is conditional and targeted. It does not show that presentism
is false, that eternalism is true, or that Brading's subsystem presents are
incoherent. It shows that, applied to the totality, the law-constitutive
account owes an account of what fixes the stages of the whole, and that by
the account's own standards current physics does not supply one. If the debt
cannot be paid, two options remain. One is to accept that the universe's
present is its whole history, which Brading says returns us to the block
universe. The other is to restrict P1\textsuperscript{**} to proper
subsystems. That is coherent, but it leaves a view on which the totality is a
four-dimensional unity; what survives is a dynamical account of the temporal
extent of subsystems that an eternalist can accept. The argument thus arrives
at Savitt's verdict by an independent route, and it relocates the empirical
question on which Brading rests her view: not only whether there are genuine
subsystems, but whether the system of the world has a true time.

Two contrasts locate the argument. Gödel objected that if time's lapse
depended on cosmic time, it would depend on ``the particular way in which
matter and its motion are arranged in the world'' \citep[p.~562]{Godel1949}, as quoted by \citet[sec.~6]{Monton2006}. Contingency of that kind is no objection
to Brading, whose view is avowedly empirical; the objection here concerns not
whether the universe's time is contingent but whether it belongs to the
universe at all (\S\ref{sec:godel}). And Wüthrich's Lorentz-symmetry objection
to Monton's foliation presentism does not touch Brading's local presents, but
it returns at the universe (\S\ref{sec:mw}).

\section{Law-constitutive presentism}\label{sec:view}

Brading's starting point is a reading of Newton. On the law-constitutive
approach, ``to be a physical thing, be it simple or composite, is (in part) to
conserve total quantity of motion (when isolated from other physical
things)'' \citep[p.~1105]{Brading2013}. A collection of interacting bodies
``forms a composite whole in virtue of conserving its total quantity of
motion and its overall direction of motion,'' a principle of unity ``for a
composite system, when that system as a whole is free from external
interactions'' \citep[p.~16]{Brading2015}. The approach is offered as ``a
principle of ontological unity that applies across the board to simple
things, composite systems, and component parts of those systems, and which
applies all the way up to the material universe as a whole. Insofar as what
exists is what is one, the law-constitutive approach to unity is a
metaphysical thesis about what exists'' \citep[p.~16]{Brading2015}.

Spacetime is demoted to an epistemic role: it ``provide[s] the framework
within which we organize our knowledge of the not-here and/or not-now''
\citep[p.~17]{Brading2015}. The present is relocated accordingly. It is
``the spatiotemporal region that is dynamically necessary to sustain'' a
unity, so that ``what is `present' depends on what is real (i.e.\ what exists,
the genuine unities), and what is real depends on the dynamical laws''
\citep[p.~17]{Brading2015}. Such a present ``is extended in time just as it is
extended in space'': ``there is no attempt to provide a unique foliation of
this region into `spatial' and `temporal' components'' (ibid.). Being present
``is relative to the dynamical system in question'' and ``is not transitive
among systems and their subsystems'' (ibid.). The 2013 paper puts the
quantity at issue as ``the spatiotemporal extent of the sufficient dynamical
ground of a given unity,'' which ``might turn out to be much, much smaller than
the abstract spatiotemporal structure within which the evolution of that
unity is completely described, or it might be the size of the block
universe'' \citep[p.~1109]{Brading2013}.

The position is stated as follows:

\begin{quote}
(P1\textsuperscript{**}) For each and every thing that exists, that thing
exists only presently, where for each thing the spatiotemporal extent of the
present for that thing is dependent on the dynamics and is (a) less than the
entire spatiotemporal evolution of the universe as represented in Minkowski
spacetime, and (b) less than the entire spatiotemporal evolution of that thing
as represented in Minkowski spacetime \citep[p.~17]{Brading2015}.
\end{quote}

``In order to avoid the block universe,'' she adds, ``all we need is (a)''
(ibid.). If a system's sustaining region ``is the entire spatiotemporal
history of the universe \ldots\ we are back to the block universe'' (ibid.).
Whether there are genuine subsystems ``smaller in spatiotemporal extent than
the entirety of spacetime'' is ``an empirical question, dependent on the
details of the dynamical laws''; ``only if there are such can the version of
presentism offered here survive'' \citep[p.~18]{Brading2015}.

Three further features matter below. First, local presents do not lead to
solipsism, because ``we belong to the same world as whatever we interact with,
and the rest of that world is as real as we are. The dynamics grounds the unity
of what there is, both of the parts and of the whole (consisting of interacting
parts), and this is what prevents the presentist from becoming a solipsist''
\citep[p.~1110]{Brading2013}. Second, given genuine subsystems, ``the present
is (at least in the first instance) local, not global'' (ibid.); the qualifier
leaves room for a global present at some level. Third, Brading has a worked-out
view of what it would be for a system to have a time of its own. Reading the
scholium to the \emph{Principia}, she argues that ``just as true motion is
unique and proper to the body (or system of bodies) in question, in contrast to
being a property of the appearances, so too true time is unique and proper to
the body (or system of bodies) in question, a property of the body or system
itself rather than of the appearances'' \citep[p.~20]{Brading2017}. Her
illustration is the Earth--Sun--Moon system. The apparent motions of Sun and
Moon can each serve as a clock, ``[b]ut these `clocks' do not tick regularly
with respect to one another, and therefore the true time of the Earth--Sun--Moon
system (if any such exists) remains to be determined'' \citep[p.~21]{Brading2017}.
Newton's project needs such a time: ``there must be a unique time parameter
proper to the system of the world, for if there is not, then a second `time'
might give different conclusions concerning the motions and forces''
\citep[p.~27]{Brading2017}. Whether one exists is open: ``it is a contingent
matter whether any such true motions exist,'' and the questions whether time is
absolute, true or mathematical ``have become empirically tractable''
\citep[p.~28]{Brading2017}.

The 2017 discussion is not about presentism, and nothing below attributes to
Brading a view about the universe's present. The argument uses her account of
true time as the standard by which, on her own terms, a time could count as the
universe's.

\section{The universe as a thing}\label{sec:unity}

The argument needs one premise.

\begin{quote}
(U) The material universe is a genuine unity, and therefore an existing thing
within the scope of P1\textsuperscript{**}.
\end{quote}

The scope claim quoted above gives the principle's intended reach, not a
verdict; a principle of unity can apply to a collection and find that it is
not one. The verdict has textual support elsewhere. The dynamics grounds the
unity ``both of the parts and of the whole (consisting of interacting parts)''
\citep[p.~1110]{Brading2013}. A 2013 footnote uses ``principle of unity'' to
``cover the unity of simple things, composite things, and the totality of
existing things,'' notes that ``[d]ifferent principles of unity might be used
for the different cases,'' and adds: ``One feature of the law-constitutive
approach discussed here is that it applies to all three''
\citep[p.~1105, n.~3]{Brading2013}. The unity of the whole is also what does
the anti-solipsist work: we belong to one world because the dynamics unifies
it.

The unity criterion supports (U) as well. A composite is one when it conserves
its total quantity of motion while ``free from external interactions.'' The
universe has no external interactions. A closed distribution of matter in
Minkowski spacetime with finite total energy--momentum conserves it. So, on
its face, the universe passes the test. That is my application of her
criterion, not her statement. A defender may deny (U); I return to that option
in \S\ref{sec:exits}, where it turns out to carry the main cost.

\section{Location, shape, and the universe's stages}\label{sec:shape}

\subsection{Two problems}

Savitt's location problem arises for every system: each stage of a system's
history has a sustaining region, and the proposal does not say which is
present. I set it aside. Grant, for the sake of argument, that Brading can
answer it, for instance by holding that each sustaining region is present for
the stage of the system that it sustains, and that there is no further fact.
The question here is what the stages of the universe are.

For a subsystem the question has a natural answer. An atom's sustaining
region is fixed by the atom's world-tube and the timescale of its constitutive
dynamics, approximately in its own rest frame. Its present is a region of
spacetime, with no preferred split into space and time, exactly as Brading
says. Nothing global is required to say which region it is.

For the universe, two readings of ``sufficient dynamical ground'' are
available.

\emph{The constitutive reading.} The ground of a whole consisting of
interacting parts is the region containing the interactions that make it one.
For the universe these are all interactions among all its parts, throughout
its history. The ground is the whole history $\W$.

\emph{The stage reading.} The ground of the universe at a stage is a region
sufficient, given the laws, for the dynamics of the whole at that stage. With
deterministic dynamics a thickened Cauchy surface will do. Such regions are
proper parts of $\W$. But unlike a subsystem's, they cannot be read off a
world-tube: a stage of the universe has no boundary in space, so it must be
bounded by global surfaces. There are as many candidate families of stages as
there are ways of slicing the history. Which surfaces bound a stage of the
universe is the \emph{shape problem}.

The argument needs one further premise, and I state it explicitly:

\begin{quote}
(S) If the universe's present is a proper region, it is one member of a
family of regions, selected by the dynamics of the whole, that orders the
universe's history into stages.
\end{quote}

Given (S), the two readings are connected. If the dynamics of the universe
selects no family of stages, the stage reading identifies no proper region as
the universe's present, and the only reading that identifies any region at all
is the constitutive one, which gives $\W$. So, given (S),
P1\textsuperscript{**}(a), applied to the universe, requires a dynamically
selected family of global stages. It does not require a preferred foliation of
spacetime for the purposes of subsystems, and it does not require the universe's
present to be thin. It requires that the dynamics of the whole determine what a
stage of the whole is.

Premise (S) is not trivial, and the conclusion is no stronger than it. A
defender may deny it by offering a proper, dynamically constituted region that
is the universe's present without being one of a sequence of stages. The next
paragraph gives the reason to accept (S); a defender who rejects it owes a
positive account of which such region is the universe's present and why.

Could the defender instead count every such slab as a present of the
universe? Then clause (a) is satisfied, but emptily. In any globally
hyperbolic spacetime, eternalist or not, every event lies in many thickened
Cauchy surfaces, and these cross one another in every direction; they do not
order the universe's history into a succession of stages at all. A
subsystem's sustaining regions at least form a sequence along its world-tube,
which is why Savitt's problem for subsystems is one of location. The
universe's candidate presents form no sequence, so ``the universe exists only
presently'' would deny nothing that a block theorist asserts. Clause (a) can do
the anti-block work Brading assigns it only if some principle picks out which
proper regions are the universe's presents; ordering by stages is the natural
candidate, and the one the problem of time concerns.

\subsection{Special relativity}

In special relativity the requirement can be met. A closed distribution of
matter with timelike total energy--momentum $P$ has a rest frame, and the
hyperplanes orthogonal to $P$ give a family of global stages fixed by the
dynamics of the whole. The universe's present is then a slab bounded by two
such hyperplanes. This fixes the orientation of the slab, not its thickness. For a
subsystem the thickness is set by the timescale of its constitutive dynamics; for
the universe nothing obviously plays that role, and I leave the question open. The
location problem is left where it was.

The price is a global present for the universe alone. Brading rejects ``a
spatially global present common to all systems'' \citep[p.~17]{Brading2015},
but presentness is system-relative and non-transitive, and the present is local
only ``in the first instance.'' A global present for the whole, coexisting with
non-transitive local presents for its parts, is not excluded by her text. She
would need to say how the universe's slab relates to the presents of its
subsystems, but the cost is modest. (If the matter content of Minkowski
spacetime is infinite and homogeneous, $P$ is undefined and this solution
fails; I set that case aside.) In Brading's own setting, then, the universe case
does not refute the view. It becomes pressing when the universe is treated, as
it is in general relativity, as a dynamical system whose spacetime geometry is
itself part of the dynamics.

\subsection{What general relativity removes}

In canonical general relativity the Hamiltonian of a spatially closed universe
is a sum of constraints that vanishes on solutions. There is no external time,
and ``nothing seems to happen'' until a time is extracted from the variables
themselves \citep[sec.~1]{Thiemann2006}. There is no asymptotic region and,
generically, no time-translation symmetry, so there is no total energy--momentum
whose rest frame could fix the stages of the whole. The special-relativistic
solution has no counterpart. A family of stages must be given by a time
function, and the question is whether the universe's dynamics fixes one.

Brading's account of true time says what would count. Three conditions can be
read off the passages in \S\ref{sec:view}. A time is the universe's own only if
it is
\begin{itemize}
\item[(T1)] \emph{proper}: fixed by the dynamics of the material universe
itself, ``a property of the body or system itself rather than of the
appearances'';
\item[(T2)] \emph{unique}: not one of several candidates that ``do not tick
regularly with respect to one another,'' since otherwise ``a second `time'
might give different conclusions'';
\item[(T3)] \emph{empirically answerable}: its existence is ``a contingent
matter,'' to be settled by the dynamics, not by stipulation.
\end{itemize}
There is also a link to the unity criterion. Newton's true time is the parameter
in which the first law holds, that is, in which an isolated body conserves its
quantity of motion. In Hamiltonian terms, the natural analogue for an isolated
system is a time relative to which the generator of its evolution is conserved:
a Hamiltonian without explicit time dependence. This regimentation is mine,
and it is the most contestable bridge in the argument.\footnote{Brading's
``quantity of motion'' is Newtonian momentum, not energy. On a momentum reading
the conservation test does no work for a spatially closed universe, whose
momentum constraints make its total momentum vanish identically. The argument of
\S\ref{sec:routes} rests chiefly on (T1) and (T2), which do not depend on this
reading; only the subsidiary point about time-dependent Hamiltonians in
\S\ref{sec:internal} does.} If it is accepted, the universe passes Brading's
unity test only relative to a time in which its own generator of evolution is
conserved, and true time and unity stand or fall together.

So the universe's present requires that a spatially closed universe have a
true time. That is the problem of time, posed in Brading's terms.

\section{Four answers from canonical gravity and cosmology}\label{sec:routes}

Isham distinguishes two broad strategies: reduce the theory to true canonical
form by choosing a time before quantizing, or quantize all variables and impose
the constraints, leading to the Wheeler--DeWitt equation
\citep[pp.~196--198]{Isham1991}. Together with the cosmological literature these
give four answers.

\subsection{Internal times: apparent times}\label{sec:internal}

Choose a time variable from the gravitational or matter degrees of freedom and
solve the constraint for its conjugate momentum, which becomes the reduced
Hamiltonian. In closed Friedmann--Lemaître--Robertson--Walker minisuperspace,
Isham does this for the radius of the universe, for its conjugate momentum and
for a scalar field. Among the general problems he lists that ``[t]he reduced
Hamiltonian is time dependent,'' and comments:

\begin{quote}
A time-dependent Hamiltonian means that energy can be fed into, or taken out
of, the quantum system. In a normal physical situation this arises because the
system is not closed, and then the time-dependence of the Hamiltonian is
determined by the environment. However, a compact three-manifold $\Sigma$ (the
``universe'') has no external environment, and so the time-dependence is
perhaps a little odd \citep[p.~200]{Isham1991}.
\end{quote}

He adds: ``There is no natural choice for the time variable and no reason to
suppose that different choices will give equivalent quantum theories'' (ibid.).

In Brading's terms these are apparent times. There are several candidate clocks,
each a feature of the universe's evolution, and they do not tick regularly with
respect to one another; the quantum theories built on them need not even agree.
This is the Earth--Sun--Moon situation at cosmological scale, and it fails (T2).
On the energy regimentation, it also fails the unity test: relative to any such
time the universe's generator of evolution is not conserved, so relative to that
time the universe is not exhibited as the kind of isolated whole that conserves
its total quantity of motion. What Isham calls ``a little odd'' is, for Brading,
a sign that the parameter is not the universe's true time.

One internal time is not merely one candidate among many. A constant-mean-curvature
(York) time, where such a foliation exists, is fixed by the geometry of the
spacetime itself rather than chosen from among competing clocks, so it is a
serious candidate for (T1) and (T2). Its reduced Hamiltonian is again time
dependent \citep[p.~274]{Wuthrich2010}. On the energy reading it therefore fails
the unity test; on the momentum reading it does not, and the argument must concede
that constant-mean-curvature time is then a live candidate for the universe's true
time. Its scope is limited, since such foliations need not exist
\citep[pp.~269--275]{Wuthrich2010}, but where it exists the shape problem has an
answer.

\subsection{Reference matter: a true time for an enlarged system}\label{sec:reference}

The time dependence is not universal. In Isham's scalar-field example the
reduced Hamiltonian,
$H_\phi=\pm\bigl(a^2\pi_a^2/12+12a^4-2a^6V(t)\bigr)^{1/2}$
\citep[eq.~5.2.8]{Isham1991}, has no explicit time dependence when the potential
vanishes. Thiemann generalizes the point: if the Lagrangian of a scalar field
coupled to general relativity contains only derivatives, the theory
deparametrizes completely, and there is ``a distinguished notion of time
generated by an invariant, positive and time independent physical
Hamiltonian'' \citep[p.~20]{Thiemann2006}. Here (T2) and the conservation test
are both satisfied.

The cost is in the clock. To keep the physical Hamiltonian close to that of
standard cosmology, the clock Lagrangian must be of Dirac--Born--Infeld type,
which for small gradients is a massless field ``with the wrong sign, i.e.\ it is a
phantom''; ``non-trivial potentials are forbidden'' \citep[p.~5]{Thiemann2006}.
The phantom's negative energy must be compensated by other matter, and
Thiemann's concrete model recollapses \citep[pp.~9--10]{Thiemann2006}. He
expects that ``whatever matter is used for deparametrisation, there will be
corrections to the standard FRW equations'' \citep[p.~9]{Thiemann2006}.

That expectation is too strong as an empirical cost. Using pressureless dust as
the clock, in the manner of \citet{BrownKuchar1995}, Giesel, Hofmann, Thiemann
and Winkler obtain a physical Hamiltonian that ``is simply the spatial integral
of the energy density,'' with evolution equations that match Einstein's ``up to
modifications proportional to the energy momentum density'' of the dust, so that
``the influence of the dust can be tuned away arbitrarily''
\citep[p.~4]{GHTW2007a}. On a flat Friedmann--Lemaître--Robertson--Walker
background the resulting equations ``formally agree with the standard FRW
equations'' \citep[p.~11]{GHTW2007b}. What survives is the character of the
clock. The dust ``must be a `phantom dust','' since with ordinary dust ``the
physical Hamiltonian would come out negative definite rather than positive
definite. Or equivalently, physical time would run backwards rather than
forward''; its energy density can be arbitrarily small but ``must not vanish''
\citep[p.~4]{GHTW2007a}. None of this depends on spatial flatness.\footnote{The
framework of Giesel and co-authors is set up for a three-manifold ``of arbitrary
topology,'' and the case without boundary, which includes the spatially closed
universe, is their default \citep[pp.~12, 36]{GHTW2007a}; Thiemann's phantom
scalar likewise works for compact space without boundary
\citep[p.~26]{Thiemann2006}. In Isham's closed model the point is elementary. Add
homogeneous comoving dust with proper time $T$ and conjugate momentum $P_T$. For
homogeneous dust the Brown--Kuchař energy density reduces to $|P_T|$, so the
constraint (5.2.5) becomes $C(a,\pi_a,\phi,\pi_\phi)\pm P_T=0$, where $C$ is
Isham's left-hand side, curvature term included. The physical Hamiltonian
$\mp P_T=C$ contains no $T$, for $k=1$ as for $k=0$, and the sign is the one
Giesel and co-authors show forces phantom dust. This extension to the closed
case is mine; it covers the homogeneous background, not perturbations.}

This answer gives a unique time with a conserved generator, but it fails (T1).
The time is proper to the universe \emph{together with} a reference medium of
negative energy, posited to serve as clocks and rods, whose observable trace can
be made as small as one likes. Brading's question was whether the system of the
world has a true time; this answer gives one to a different system. The analogy
with Newton is exact: one does not discover the true time of the solar system by
adding a body whose motion is uniform by construction.

The answer has an honest empirical form, and Brading's empiricism should take it
seriously. If such a medium existed, it would simply be part of the material
universe, and the universe would then have a true time. The route therefore
turns into an empirical hypothesis: there is a pervasive phantom reference
medium. Nothing in current cosmology supports it, and because its influence can
be tuned away, observation constrains it only weakly. A presentism that needs it
has acquired a specific and unusual empirical commitment.

\subsection{Cosmic time: a mathematical time from an idealized model}\label{sec:cosmic}

Bamonti and Thébault defend a conservative alternative. They read the time in
the Friedmann equations as proper time along the Hubble flow, in synchronous
gauge, so that the equations describe the correlation between two partial
observables, the Hubble parameter and cosmic time
\citep[secs.~4--5]{BamontiThebault2025}. On their own account, ``there is no
physical system which even approximately measures proper time along the Hubble
flow'' \citep[p.~5]{BamontiThebault2025}. Cosmic time is recovered as a
model-based global average, with the clock hypothesis functioning as a
coordinative definition in Reichenbach's sense
\citep[pp.~19--20]{BamontiThebault2025}. Nothing here criticizes their proposal,
which is about the interpretation of cosmology, not about presentism.

In Brading's taxonomy, cosmic time is a \emph{mathematical} time: a metrical
parameter constructed, by idealization, for the purposes of theory, as the
astronomers' equation of time corrected apparent solar time. That it is kept by
no material clock does not disqualify it. Brading is explicit that ``there may be
no material clock that ticks precisely in accordance with the metric of time,''
while ``we have good empirical reasons'' for holding that time flows equably
\citep[p.~37]{Brading2017}; the time of astronomy ``is mathematical, and is
neither relative nor apparent'' \citep[p.~25]{Brading2017}.
So this answer is the defender's strongest. What it still owes is an argument that
the time is proper to the actual universe and unique: that the idealization and
global averaging by which cosmic time is recovered from an inhomogeneous universe
are fixed by its dynamics rather than chosen, which is what Bamonti and Thébault's
description of the clock hypothesis as a coordinative definition calls into
question. I take this to be open. Implemented instead by deparametrizing with the comoving matter, whose
proper time is cosmic time, this answer becomes an instance of
\S\ref{sec:reference}; the actual comoving matter cannot play the role directly,
since it has positive energy and ordinary dust would make physical time run
backwards \citep[p.~4]{GHTW2007a}.

\subsection{Gödel, Newton and contingency}\label{sec:godel}

It is worth separating this objection from Gödel's. Gödel argued that since some
general-relativistic universes admit no cosmic time, anyone who holds that time
lapses in ours ``accepts as a consequence that, whether or not an objective lapse
of time exists \ldots\ depends on the particular way in which matter and its
motion are arranged in the world,'' a consequence that ``can hardly be considered
as satisfactory'' \citep[p.~562]{Godel1949}, as quoted by \citet[sec.~6]{Monton2006}.
Monton replies that a presentist need not hold that the same foliation exists in
all physically possible worlds, and he cites Belot and Earman's observation that,
in constant-mean-curvature quantum gravity, ``the time which results \ldots\ is
certainly not the absolute time of Newton'' \citep[sec.~6]{Monton2006}.

Brading can say more than Monton. Her view is empirical through and through: that
the present depends on the dynamics is its thesis, and that true time might not
exist is, for her, a contingent matter to be settled empirically. So Gödel's
contingency is no objection to her. The objection of this section is different.
It does not say that cosmic time is contingent on the arrangement of matter. It
says that, on the best current account of it, cosmic time is not kept by the
matter at all, and that the times that are kept by matter belong either to many
competing clocks or to an added reference medium.

\subsection{No time}\label{sec:notime}

If no time is extracted, the constraints are imposed on the states and the
universe is described by a solution of the Wheeler--DeWitt equation with no
evolution parameter \citep[sec.~5.3]{Isham1991}. The universe may then count as a
conserved whole, but it has no stages. P1\textsuperscript{**} is then false or
undefined for it: there is no proper region that is its present. Whether such a
formalism leaves room for change at all is a separate and contested question; see
\citet{Earman2002} and the reply by \citet{Maudlin2002}.

\section{Monton, Wüthrich and the local present}\label{sec:mw}

The debate over presentism and quantum gravity has concerned a global present.
Monton grants that presentism is incompatible with special and general
relativity and argues that it is compatible with quantum theories of gravity that
use a fixed foliation, such as constant-mean-curvature canonical gravity, on a
theoretical hypothesis that one leaf represents the existing events
\citep{Monton2006}. Wüthrich replies that fixed-foliation theories break a
Lorentz symmetry that is ``fantastically well confirmed,'' and that the
constant-mean-curvature approach is limited in scope and does not by itself
supply a present that underwrites becoming \citep[pp.~269--275]{Wuthrich2010}. He
also notes the time dependence of the reduced Hamiltonian for this choice of time
\citep[p.~274]{Wuthrich2010}; constant-mean-curvature time is an internal time in
the sense of \S\ref{sec:internal}.

Brading's view is designed to stay out of that exchange. It posits no foliation,
and Wüthrich's classification of presentist responses to special
relativity---frame-relative existence, the point present and the past-light-cone
present \citep[pp.~263--264]{Wuthrich2010}---does not include a present fixed by a
system's dynamics. For subsystems, the Lorentz-symmetry objection does not apply.
Does it apply to the universe? Not automatically. Every answer of
\S\ref{sec:routes} that gives the universe stages gives it a global time function,
but a dynamical reference frame is not a violation of Lorentz symmetry. In the
Brown--Kuchař construction the dust selects a frame within a generally covariant
theory \citep{BrownKuchar1995}, and the laws violate no symmetry that GR respects.
What carries over from the Monton--Wüthrich exchange is narrower: if the universe's
present is tied to the stages of such a frame, the present has a preferred global
foliation at the level of ontology, though not of law. The empirical objection
then lapses, and the philosophical question Wüthrich presses about fixed foliations
remains: why should that frame's stages, rather than the representation-dependent
stages of any other, mark what exists?

\section{The exits}\label{sec:exits}

\paragraph{The universe's present is its whole history.} A defender may accept the
constitutive reading. Then clause (a) is false of the universe, and in Brading's
words ``we are back to the block universe'' \citep[p.~17]{Brading2015}. This is
not her defeat condition, which describes the case in which ``the only dynamical
system that there is, is this entirety, and there are no genuine subsystems''
\citep[p.~1109]{Brading2013}. It is compatible with genuine subsystems. The block
returns at the top: every event belongs to the universe's history, so every event
lies within the present of an existing thing, and P1\textsuperscript{**} excludes
no event from what exists.

\paragraph{Restrict P1\textsuperscript{**} to proper subsystems.} A defender may
instead deny (U), or deny that the universe is the kind of thing that has a
present, and confine P1\textsuperscript{**} to proper subsystems. This is
coherent, and I think it is the defender's best option. Its cost is what it leaves
standing. The universe is either not a unity or a unity that is not confined to a
present. If it is not a unity, Brading needs another account of what makes the
world one, since it is the dynamical unity of ``the whole (consisting of
interacting parts)'' that keeps the local present from collapsing into solipsism
\citep[p.~1110]{Brading2013}. If it is a unity not confined to a present, then
among the things that exist is a four-dimensional whole whose extent is the
entire history. Either way, ``all we need is (a)'' no longer suffices to avoid
the block universe. Clause (a) for subsystems says that no proper subsystem is
present throughout the block. That is compatible with an eternalist ontology of
events on which every subsystem is temporally bounded, and it is compatible with
the union of the subsystems' presents being the whole history.\footnote{For any
two proper regions whose union is $\W$, a system with each as its present satisfies
(a) and, with histories chosen accordingly, (b); per-system properness says
nothing about the union.} What survives is a dynamical account of the temporal
extent of subsystems: valuable, but, in Savitt's phrase, ``a characterization of a
relativistic present, not relativistic presentism.''

\paragraph{Find the true time.} The most interesting exit is Brading's own
empiricism. Perhaps the true time of the system of the world is yet to be found:
in a fixed-foliation quantum gravity, in an actual reference medium, or in
something not yet proposed. Brading suggests Brown's dynamical approach to the
chronometric significance of the metric as a natural starting point for a
law-constitutive presentism beyond special relativity \citep[p.~18]{Brading2015};
see \citet{Brown2005}. That approach explains the behaviour of local rods and
clocks from the dynamics of matter. It does not, so far as I can see, select
global stages for a closed universe, but that is a question worth pursuing on
Brading's behalf. If this exit is taken, the universe case does not refute the
view; it relocates its decisive test. The empirical hypothesis then depends not
only on whether there are genuine subsystems, as Brading says, but on whether the
system of the world has a true time.

\section{Objections and limits}\label{sec:limits}

\paragraph{Brading's view is special-relativistic.} Her texts work in Minkowski
spacetime, and the argument agrees that there the universe case is manageable
(\S\ref{sec:shape}). The general-relativistic step is an extension. Its bridge
premises are the stage reading of ``sufficient dynamical ground'' for the
universe, and the conditions (T1)--(T3) drawn from her 2017 account of true time.
A defender may reject either. Rejecting the first returns the constitutive
reading and the block at the top; rejecting the second requires a different
account of when a time belongs to a system.

\paragraph{The present is a region, not a slice.} Agreed. Nothing above requires
the universe's present to be thin or to be a leaf of a foliation. It requires
only that the dynamics determine what a stage of the whole is. A slab is a region;
the question is what bounds it.

\paragraph{Momentum, not energy.} If the unity test is read as momentum
conservation, the subsidiary point about time-dependent Hamiltonians lapses. The
main point, that no time is unique and proper to the material universe, does not
depend on it.

\paragraph{The answers may not be exhaustive.} The argument claims only that the
answers in the cited literature each fail to supply a true time of the material
universe. A new answer would meet the argument's own standard, and would count, on
Brading's empiricism, as evidence for her view.

\paragraph{Scope of the physics.} Isham's examples are closed minisuperspace
models. The dust and phantom-scalar constructions hold for closed spatial
topology, but their cosmological comparisons use flat backgrounds, and the
closed-model extension above covers only the homogeneous background. Bamonti and
Thébault do not discuss conservation in synchronous gauge. Each is a point at
which further physics could weaken the argument.

\paragraph{Priority.} No published statement of this argument was found in the
sources read: Brading 2013, 2015 and 2017; Savitt 2021; Monton 2006 (2005
preprint); Wüthrich 2010; Isham 1991 (pp.~192--202); Thiemann 2006; and Bamonti
and Thébault 2025, all at full-text depth. Balaguer's \emph{How to Be a Presentist}
\citep{Balaguer2026} and Mason's empirical presentism \citep{Mason2026} were read
only at abstract depth, as were Earman 2002 and Maudlin 2002; Giesel, Hofmann,
Thiemann and Winkler \citep{GHTW2007a,GHTW2007b} at the passages cited; Gödel 1949
and Belot and Earman 2001 only as quoted by Monton. A prior statement would defeat
the priority claim, not the argument.

\section{Conclusion}\label{sec:conclusion}

Brading's principle of unity is offered for the whole of what exists, up to the
material universe. Taken at that scale, P1\textsuperscript{**} needs an account of
the universe's own present, and the universe's present needs an account of what a
stage of the universe is. In special relativity the universe's total
energy--momentum supplies one, at the modest price of a global present for the
whole alone. In a spatially closed general-relativistic universe the stages of the
whole must be fixed by a time that is, in Brading's own terms, unique and proper to
the system of the world. The answers in the literature give apparent times that
disagree, a true time for the universe plus a posited phantom medium, a
mathematical time whose claim to be the universe's own is open, a
constant-mean-curvature time that survives if the unity test is read as momentum
conservation, or no time. Unless one of the open candidates succeeds, or the
defender rejects premise (S) and says which proper region is the universe's
present, the only reading that selects a region gives the whole history, and by
Brading's criterion that is the block. Restricting P1\textsuperscript{**} to subsystems
avoids the block for the parts but not for the whole. The law-constitutive account
therefore owes what it does not yet give: a true time for the universe, or an
account of the world's unity that does without one.
